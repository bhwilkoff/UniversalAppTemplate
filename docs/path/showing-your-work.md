# Showing your work

**Where you are.** You have finished a stage, or a round of one, and its
last line asks you to be ready to show something. This page is about
what happens next: who sees it, what they say back, and how to get that
conversation, in a cohort or on your own.

In 2006 I wrote a lesson post to my eighth graders that ended the way
every stage of this path ends:

> Discuss with at least two people your list of expectations. Try to
> agree on the two that you think are most essential. Be ready to share
> them out.

That last sentence is there because a way of working is learned by
showing it to someone, not by reading about it. It was true of a list of
classroom expectations, and it is just as true of an app built with an
agent.

**Every round ends with someone else seeing it.**

## What I want

I want every round of work to end with another person looking at it and
saying something you can use next. Not praise, and not a grade. The
research on feedback is unusually clear about what helps: feedback works
when it says where you are going, how it is going, and what to try next.
And yet, in more than a third of the cases studied, feedback made the
work worse, mostly when it turned into a comment on the person rather
than the work. The same research found that giving feedback teaches the
giver as much as getting it teaches the receiver, which is why the path
asks you to show your work to people rather than hand it in.

## Explain one decision, with the agent closed

Before anyone says anything about what you showed, take two minutes,
with your agent closed, to explain one decision behind it: why this
source, why this floor, why that line and not another. Explain the
decision, not the code. The agent wrote the code, and nobody needs you
to recite it, but the decision was supposed to be yours.

The studies of people learning to build with AI agree on where the loss
comes from: handing over the understanding while feeling as if you
learned it. The newest builders lose the most, and notice it the least.
Saying a decision out loud, without the agent there to say it for you,
is a quick way to find out whether the understanding is still yours.

If you cannot explain it, nothing has gone wrong. You have found your
next prompt, and it might sound like this:

> Before we change anything else, explain why we decided to do it this
> way, in plain words, and tell me what we gave up.

Then correct anything in the answer that does not match what you know.

## Three questions for every bring-back

Once you have explained your decision, the people watching answer three
questions, out loud or in a line each, while you listen:

1. **Where is it going?** What the app is for, as they understood it
   from what you showed them. If their answer is not your "why we build"
   paragraph, that is worth knowing.
2. **How is it going?** One thing they saw work, and one thing they saw
   that did not.
3. **What is next?** One thing they would try.

Then you say, in a sentence, what you will do next, and you write it in
your note under "Next:". Every stage's "Be ready to..." line is built to
fit this shape. What it asks you to show is how it is going, the
decision it asks you to explain is where you are taking it, and what
you say last is what comes next. "Great job," on its own, answers none of the three questions, so
when you hear it, ask for one thing that worked and one that did not.

## In a cohort

In a cohort, you show your work to your group, the same partners from
week to week, so the people answering the three questions are people who saw
last week's version. Your teacher gives feedback too, and `COURSE.md`
says how each live session makes room for all of it.

## On your own

Most people who build from this template will not be in a cohort, and
the same feedback is still within reach. These three work best
together, because each one sees something the others cannot.

- **A friend as a partner.** Ask one person to open your app's address
  on their own phone once a stage, to listen while you explain one
  decision, and to answer the three questions. They do not need to know
  anything about building software, because the questions are about the
  app, not the code. If they are building something too, trade.
- **The human-shaped review, labeled as AI.** Ask your agent for a
  human-shaped review ([Talking to your agent](talking-to-your-agent.md)
  has the details). It asks the questions this method asks, says plainly
  what it could not see, and never gives a grade. It opens with a line
  saying an AI wrote it, so if you share it, it arrives as what it is.
  It cannot hold your phone or watch a person use your app, so it adds
  to a person's feedback rather than taking its place.
- **A public post.** Post your app where its people already are, with
  the link, one screenshot, and one question you actually want
  answered. The day after I posted Archive Watch on Reddit, I asked the
  agent to use the comments to find issues across every platform (stage
  06 has the prompt). Copy what people say into your note first, decide
  what you think of it, and only then bring it to the agent.

## What it costs

Showing unfinished work is uncomfortable, and it is supposed to be
unfinished. On Archive Watch's third day, the commits run from "UI
feedback pass" to "UI feedback round 7", and every one of those rounds
was me sending back a list of what was still wrong. A friend's time is
a real thing to ask for, so give something back, the way stage 07 asks
you to with anyone who shapes your app. And a public post brings in strangers, not all of them kind, so
you decide which comments get an answer and which only get read.

Who will see your next round?
