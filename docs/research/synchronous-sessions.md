# Session research: studio sessions for a cohort of app builders

Researched 2026-11-18 for the humanshaped.org cohorts (4 to 8 adults, each building their own app with an AI coding agent; 60 minutes weekly in Google Meet with the add-on's main stage and side panel).

The problem it answers: sessions that read as generic claims ("Your agent's word is not proof"), questions that are really instructions ("A question for everyone: write your next prompt"), and a plan invented fresh each week. What the sources below agree on is a fixed weekly routine built around each person's real work. Each week the maker brings an artifact and a question of their own, peers use the work and report what they saw before they judge it, and every session closes with a written next action that gets checked the following week.

**How verified.** "Opened" means I read the primary page or document during this session. "Search only" means I confirmed the citation from bibliographic records or reliable secondary pages but did not read the source myself. UNVERIFIED means I could not confirm the claim or the source. No citation here was made up.

## 1. Formats for sessions where each person builds their own project

### 1.1 Liz Lerman's Critical Response Process (CRP)
**Idea.** Feedback on work in progress runs in four steps in a fixed order: (1) statements of meaning (what stood out to responders), (2) the maker asks questions, (3) responders ask *neutral* questions, meaning questions with no opinion inside them, and (4) permissioned opinions, offered as "I have an opinion about ___, would you like to hear it?", which the maker may decline. A facilitator keeps the order.
**In a 60-minute Meet session.** One builder shares their live app on the stage. Peers have already tried it. Step 1 happens in the panel, where each peer writes one thing that struck them while using it, and the posts appear on the stage. In step 2 the maker asks the question they came with ("Did the empty state tell you what to do?"). Steps 3 and 4 are spoken. About 12 to 15 minutes for one maker.
**Citation.** Liz Lerman, "Critical Response Process," lizlerman.com, https://lizlerman.com/critical-response-process/ (opened). Book: Liz Lerman and John Borstel, *Critique Is Creative: The Critical Response Process in Theory and Action* (search only; the 2022 date and Wesleyan University Press imprint are UNVERIFIED); earlier primer by the same authors, *Critical Response Process* (2003) (search only).

### 1.2 Tuning Protocol (School Reform Initiative lineage)
**Idea.** The presenter shares context, the work, and a *focusing question*, which is posted for everyone to see. Participants ask factual clarifying questions, look closely at the work, and pause silently. Then they give "warm" feedback (where the work meets the presenter's goals) and "cool" feedback (gaps, often phrased as questions) while the presenter stays silent and takes notes. Last, the presenter reflects aloud, explicitly "not a time for the presenter to defend." Sentence starters it supplies: warm, "It seems important…", "I appreciate…", "I want to make sure to keep…"; cool, "I wonder if…", "One way to more closely align the goal is…". The full protocol takes 45 to 60 minutes.
**In Meet.** Compressed to 10 minutes for one builder: 1 minute for the focusing question (pinned on the stage), 1 for clarifying questions, 3 for everyone using the app on their own device, 1 of silent notes in the panel, 3 of warm then cool feedback with the maker muted and taking notes, and 1 for the maker's reflection.
**Citation.** Joseph McDonald (Coalition of Essential Schools), revised by David Allen, "Tuning Protocol," School Reform Initiative, PDF hosted at https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1758095874/rcboeorg/xwmvcz88wzkgost3iyqw/tuningprotocolsri.pdf (opened; the protocol has been in use since its 1992 trial). SRI listing: https://schoolreforminitiative.org/?p=12767 (redirects; not opened).

### 1.3 Design-studio and art-school crits
**Idea.** The crit is studio education's signature method. UK research found students often experience it as a test of verbal performance, and anxiety can stop them from hearing the feedback. The researchers recommend structured formats and pairing students up to review work before a whole-group crit.
**In Meet.** Never put someone in front of the whole group cold. Each builder first gets feedback from one or two peers in a breakout room, then brings one finding back to the group.
**Citation.** Margo Blythman, Susan Orr, and Bernadette Blair, *Critiquing the Crit*, Higher Education Academy project report, 2007, record at https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/7385/ (search only; no full text available; the findings come from summaries citing the report). For computing: C. D. Hundhausen, N. H. Narayanan, and M. E. Crosby, "Exploring studio-based instructional models for computing education," SIGCSE 2008 (search only; students present and justify their designs and get design critiques from peers).

### 1.4 "I Like / I Wish / What If"
**Idea.** A lightweight feedback frame from design thinking. Each person writes notes in three categories, and "What if" deliberately pushes past critique toward new possibilities.
**In Meet.** The panel shows three boxes after someone tries an app, and the maker receives the notes grouped by category. Good for pair feedback when there is no time for a full protocol.
**Citation.** Credited to the Stanford d.school by later toolkits, for example SessionLab/Hyper Island, "I Like, I Wish, I Wonder," https://www.sessionlab.com/methods/i-like-i-wish-i-wonder (search only). The original d.school method card is UNVERIFIED.

### 1.5 Gallery walk
**Idea.** Work is posted at "stations." People rotate, spend a few minutes at each, and leave written responses. It works best with open-ended prompts. Online versions post a link to each artifact and collect written responses.
**In Meet.** Each builder's live app link and a one-line "try this" appear as tiles on the stage. For 8 minutes everyone opens two assigned apps on their own phone or laptop and leaves a written note in the panel for each.
**Citation.** M. Francek, "Promoting Discussion in the Science Classroom Using Gallery Walks," *Journal of College Science Teaching*, 2006, NSTA listing https://www.nsta.org/resources/promoting-discussion-science-classroom-using-gallery-walks (search only; sources disagree on volume and pages). SERC description: https://serc.carleton.edu/sp/library/gallerywalk/index.html (search only). Online adaptation: UCF TOPR, "Digitalizing Gallery Walks," https://topr.online.ucf.edu/digitalizing-gallery-walks-a-method-for-student-centered-feedback-and-engagement/ (search only).

### 1.6 Demos and show-and-tell (Hack Club, Recurse Center)
**Idea.** Hack Club's club-leader guidance ends every meeting with 2 to 3 minute demos and explicitly welcomes unfinished work. The Recurse Center (RC) runs weekly 5-minute presentations of something you programmed, many of them off the cuff with live coding, and strongly encourages everyone to present at least once. RC's User's Manual tells people to share what they are working on "often and widely," to ask questions and admit uncertainty in public, and lists check-ins among its accountability options.
**In Meet.** Fixed 2-minute demo slots on the live app. The builder shares their own screen or phone, and the stage shows a timer. Unfinished work counts.
**Citation.** Recurse Center, "User's Manual," https://www.recurse.com/manual (opened). RC presentations: https://www.recurse.com/events/rc-january-presentations (search only). Hack Club, "Running a Meeting," https://wiki.hackclub.com/wiki/running-a-meeting (search only; the host did not resolve when fetched, so the quoted guidance comes from the search result and is UNVERIFIED as currently live).

### 1.7 Check-ins and stand-ups
**Idea.** Recursers post or say a short daily check-in about what they are working on, and participants compare it to a stand-up. The Scrum Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event.
**In Meet.** The first 5 minutes are a written check-in in the panel (what changed in my app, plus a link), shown as a grid on the stage. It is written rather than spoken, so it costs no airtime.
**Citation.** RC manual (above, opened). Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, *The Scrum Guide*, 2020, https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html (opened). That the 2020 guide dropped the "three questions" of the Daily Scrum is from memory and UNVERIFIED.

### 1.8 Pair programming and live coding in teaching
**Idea.** In UC Santa Cruz's introductory course, students who programmed in pairs stayed through the final exam at a higher rate (90.8% vs 80.4%), produced better programs, and were more confident. Carpentries "participatory live coding" means the instructor codes slowly and narrates while learners follow along, and mistakes are treated as teaching moments.
**In Meet.** Occasionally one builder "drives" their own agent session live for 5 minutes while thinking aloud, and one peer navigates, asking "what are you checking now?" Seeing a real prompt, the agent's answer, and the builder checking it on a device teaches more than a slide claiming "your agent's word is not proof."
**Citation.** Charlie McDowell, Linda Werner, Heather E. Bullock, and Julian Fernald, "Pair Programming Improves Student Retention, Confidence, and Program Quality," *Communications of the ACM* 49(8), 2006, https://cacmb4.acm.org/magazines/2006/8/5850-pair-programming-improves-student-retention-confidence-and-program-quality/fulltext (search only). The Carpentries, *Instructor Training*, "Live Coding is a Skill," https://preview.carpentries.org/instructor-training/17-live.html (opened).

### 1.9 Carpentries sticky notes
**Idea.** Each learner signals status with two colors, one for "done" and one for "stuck." The Carpentries now recommend colors other than red and green for accessibility, and suggest Zoom's reactions as the online equivalent. Minute cards collected at breaks feed back into pacing.
**In Meet.** A two-state status button in the panel ("tried it" / "stuck"), with the stage showing counts, so the leader knows when to move on without asking "is everyone done?"
**Citation.** The Carpentries, *Instructor Training*, "Working With Your Team," https://preview.carpentries.org/instructor-training/21-management.html (search only).

### 1.10 The writers' workshop
**Idea.** The Milford/Iowa model keeps the author silent while each peer gets a timed turn, and the author responds at the end. It is widely criticized: Clarion West moved away from the "gag rule" toward author statements, the author speaking, and open discussion at the end.
**In Meet.** Borrow the timed turns and the maker's closing response, but let the maker speak first with their own question. The Tuning Protocol and CRP do this better.
**Citation.** Clarion West, "Workshop Methods," https://www.clarionwest.org/resources/workshop-methods/ (search only). Clarion West, "Evolving Workshop Culture," 2022, https://www.clarionwest.org/2022/04/01/evolving-workshop-culture-2/ (search only).

## 2. Facilitating live on video

### 2.1 Community of Inquiry (Garrison, Anderson, and Archer)
**Idea.** Online learning that works needs three presences. *Social presence*: people show up as real people. *Cognitive presence*: they build meaning through sustained inquiry. *Teaching presence*: someone designs and facilitates.
**In Meet.** Teaching presence is the fixed routine and the leader's timekeeping. Social presence is seeing one another's real apps and faces. Cognitive presence is the feedback-to-next-action loop. A session that only presents claims supplies teaching presence and nothing else.
**Citation.** D. R. Garrison, T. Anderson, and W. Archer, "Critical Inquiry in a Text-Based Environment: Computer Conferencing in Higher Education," *The Internet and Higher Education* 2(2–3), 87–105, 2000, https://core.ac.uk/download/58774863.pdf (search only).

### 2.2 Zoom fatigue
**Idea.** Bailenson names four causes: eye gaze at close distance, cognitive load, an all-day mirror (seeing yourself), and reduced mobility. The paper is theoretical, not experimental.
**In Meet.** Spend the session's middle third off the main video grid: people use apps on their own devices, write in the panel, or move to small rooms. Encourage hiding self-view. Do not fill 60 minutes with stage slides.
**Citation.** Jeremy N. Bailenson, "Nonverbal Overload: A Theoretical Argument for the Causes of Zoom Fatigue," *Technology, Mind, and Behavior* 2(1), 2021, https://doi.org/10.1037/tmb0000030, PDF at https://vhil.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj29011/files/media/file/bailenson-apa-nonverbal-overload.pdf (opened; four section headings confirmed). Stanford summary: https://news.stanford.edu/2021/02/23/four-causes-zoom-fatigue-solutions/ (search only).

### 2.3 Breakout rooms
**Idea.** The evidence is small-scale and mostly self-reported. It favors groups of 2 to 3, a specific assigned task, and the instructor visiting rooms; silent, unstructured rooms are the common failure. Stachowiak's practical advice: several short breakouts instead of one long one, choose the activity first, give each group a shared editable document or slide, put a slide of instructions on screen, and monitor every group from one place.
**In Meet.** Trios, 15 to 20 minutes, with the task on the panel (not spoken) and a visible timer. The leader rotates through rooms.
**Citation.** Bonni Stachowiak, "How Do You Make Zoom Breakout Rooms Less Boring?", *Teaching in Higher Ed*, October 2, 2020, http://teachinginhighered.com/2020/10/02/how-do-you-make-zoom-breakout-rooms-less-boring/ (opened). Zouhri, "Different Zoom Breakout Room Methods and Techniques Effects on Engineering Students' Learning Outcomes," ASEE 2021, https://peer.asee.org/different-zoom-breakout-room-methods-and-techniques-effects-on-engineering-students-learning-outcomes-for-engineering-courses.pdf (search only).

### 2.4 Liberating Structures
- **1-2-4-All** (opened, http://www.liberatingstructures.com/1-2-4-all). 1 minute of silent individual writing, 2 in pairs, 5 in fours, 7 all together. *In Meet with 4 to 8 people:* use 1-2-All. One silent minute writing in the panel, 2 minutes in pairs, then the stage shows what people wrote.
- **What, So What, Now What** (opened, http://www.liberatingstructures.com/what-so-what-now-what). Three rounds: observable facts, then meaning, then actions. Each round is individual writing, small-group sharing, then plenary. *In Meet:* the debrief after people try one another's apps. "What did you do and what happened on the screen?" comes before "what does it mean?", which comes before "what will you build next?" This order is the cure for opinion-first feedback.
- **Troika Consulting** (opened, https://www.liberatingstructures.com/troika-consulting). Trios, about 30 to 35 minutes. The client states a challenge (1–2 min); consultants ask clarifying questions (1–2 min); the client turns away and listens silently while the consultants discuss ideas (4–5 min); the client shares takeaways (1–2 min); then rotate. *In Meet:* the client turns their camera off while the two consultants talk. It is a natural fit for "what should I build next?"

### 2.5 Priya Parker, *The Art of Gathering*
**Idea.** A gathering needs a specific, disputable purpose, not a category like "class." Hosts should use "generous authority" to enforce structure on behalf of guests, rather than staying "chill."
**In Meet.** State each session's purpose as a decision about the work ("leave with a tested next build step"), and enforce the timings and protocol rules on everyone's behalf.
**Citation.** Priya Parker, *The Art of Gathering: How We Meet and Why It Matters*, Riverhead, 2018 (search only; the concepts are confirmed through summaries, not the book).

### 2.6 Flower Darby, *Small Teaching Online*
**Idea.** Small, evidence-based changes to online teaching rather than course overhauls.
**Citation.** Flower Darby with James M. Lang, *Small Teaching Online: Applying Learning Science in Online Classes*, Jossey-Bass/Wiley, 2019 (search only). I could not verify specific claims about synchronous sessions; UNVERIFIED for any particular practice.

## 3. Good discussion questions versus instructions

### 3.1 The Question Formulation Technique (Right Question Institute)
**Idea.** Learners produce their own questions from a stimulus (the "QFocus," which is a statement, not a question). Rules: ask as many questions as you can; do not stop to discuss, judge, or answer; write each question exactly as stated; change any statement into a question. Then sort closed from open questions, convert some, and choose priorities.
**In Meet.** The QFocus is a real artifact, such as a screenshot of one learner's app on a phone with something broken, or a note from a tester. For 4 minutes everyone writes questions in the panel. The maker picks the two they most want answered, and those become the questions for their feedback round.
**Citation.** Right Question Institute, "What is the QFT?", https://rightquestion.org/what-is-the-qft/ (opened).

### 3.2 Stephen Brookfield
**Idea.** Discussion has to be designed; "are there any questions?" does not produce it. *Circle of Voices*: in groups of 4 to 6, everyone gets silent thinking time and then an uninterrupted turn, which stops a pecking order forming early, and the open discussion afterwards may only build on ideas already voiced. The *Critical Incident Questionnaire*, given at the end of every class, asks when you were most engaged, when you felt most distanced, which action helped most, and which puzzled you most; the leader reports themes back next time.
**In Meet.** Run Circle of Voices for the after-use round. End each session with one CIQ question in the panel and open the next session with one minute on what people said.
**Citation.** Stephen D. Brookfield and Stephen Preskill, *Discussion as a Way of Teaching: Tools and Techniques for Democratic Classrooms*, 2nd ed., Jossey-Bass, 2005 (search only). Circle of Voices summary: https://collaboration.sites.wm.edu/2019/04/12/circle-of-voices/ (search only). CIQ text: https://graduate.baylor.edu/sites/g/files/ecbvkj1751/files/2023-11/brookfields_critical_incident_questionnaire_002.pdf (search only; exact canonical wording UNVERIFIED). A specific Brookfield taxonomy of question types such as "questions that ask for more evidence" is UNVERIFIED and not used.

### 3.3 NSRF Consultancy: framing a real question
**Idea.** The presenter brings a real dilemma they have not already solved, one they can affect themselves ("If your answer is not you, you need to change your focus"), and frames it as a question. "Questions that can be answered with a 'yes' or 'no' generally provide less feedback… so avoid those." Clarifying questions are "who, what, where, when, and how," not "why." Probing questions help the presenter think, and the group then discusses while the presenter listens and takes notes.
**In Meet.** Before each session, each builder writes one focusing question about their own app in the panel. That replaces the leader writing "a question for everyone."
**Citation.** Gene Thompson-Grove, Paula Evans, and Faith Dunne, "Consultancy Protocol," National School Reform Faculty, https://www.nsrfharmony.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/consultancy_0.pdf (opened).

### 3.4 Cold call and warm call
**Idea.** In graduate accounting classes, heavy cold-calling raised *voluntary* participation and reduced discomfort, and a 2019 follow-up found women's participation rose more than men's. Lemov pairs Cold Call with "No Opt Out" inside a "high challenge, low threat" culture. "Warm call" means giving think or pair time before calling on someone.
**In Meet.** Use warm calls on names, in rotation, after written think time: "Dana, then Sam: what did you see when you tried it?" Never call on someone with an open-ended "anyone?"
**Citation.** Elise J. Dallimore, Julie H. Hertenstein, and Marjorie B. Platt, "Impact of Cold-Calling on Student Voluntary Participation," *Journal of Management Education* 37(3), 305–341, 2013 (online 2012), https://doi.org/10.1177/1052562912446067 (search only). Dallimore, Hertenstein, and Platt, "Leveling the Playing Field: How Cold-Calling Affects Class Discussion Gender Equity," *Journal of Education and Learning* 8(2), 2019 (search only). Doug Lemov, *Teach Like a Champion* (Jossey-Bass, 2010; 3.0 edition 2021) and field notes at https://teachlikeachampion.org/ (search only).

### 3.5 Wait time (Mary Budd Rowe)
**Idea.** Teachers typically wait about one second after asking a question. When the pause after the question (and after an answer) reaches about 3 seconds, students answer at greater length, talk to one another more, and speculate more. Rowe's evidence is older and largely her own synthesis.
**In Meet.** Every question on the stage comes with a visible writing pause of 30 to 60 seconds in the panel before anyone speaks (on video, a few seconds is not enough), and the leader waits after each answer.
**Citation.** Mary Budd Rowe, "Wait Time: Slowing Down May Be a Way of Speeding Up!", *Journal of Teacher Education* 37(1), 43–50, 1986, https://doi.org/10.1177/002248718603700110 (search only). Rowe, "Wait-time and rewards as instructional variables… Part one: Wait-time," *Journal of Research in Science Teaching* 11(2), 81–94, 1974, https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.3660110202 (search only).

## 4. Routines that repeat every session

### 4.1 Protocols as routines (SRI and NSRF)
**Idea.** The Tuning Protocol and Consultancy documents both say protocols are "most powerful and effective when used within an ongoing professional learning community." The same protocol every time lets people spend attention on the work instead of on the format.
**In Meet.** One feedback protocol, unchanged for all five weeks. Only the artifacts change.
**Citation.** Tuning and Consultancy PDFs above (opened).

### 4.2 Sprint Review and Retrospective (Scrum)
**Idea.** The Sprint Review is "a working session and the Scrum Team should avoid limiting it to a presentation": the team inspects the working increment and decides what comes next. The Retrospective exists "to plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness." The Definition of Done is "a formal description of the state of the Increment."
**In Meet.** Each week is a sprint. The session opens with a *review* of the real increment (the live app on a device) and closes with a short *retro* on the builder's process (how they worked with their agent), followed by the next plan.
**Citation.** Schwaber and Sutherland, *The Scrum Guide*, 2020, https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html (opened).

### 4.3 Cohort courses (altMBA, Maven)
**Idea.** altMBA sorts each cohort into learning groups of 3 to 5, reshuffled every week, who work on the week's prompt; coaches connect students rather than fixing problems for them, and students "ship" a project for each prompt. Wes Kao (Maven co-founder, altMBA's founding executive director) treats course design as a set of levers, among them project-driven work and group interaction, and argues the value of a cohort course is peers doing real work and critiquing one another.
**In Meet.** Rotate trios weekly so each builder meets everyone across five weeks. The leader connects people ("Sam hit the same sign-in problem last week") instead of answering.
**Citation.** altMBA, "FAQ," https://altmba.com/faq (search only). Wes Kao, "Course Mechanics Canvas: 12 Levers to Achieve Course-Market Fit," https://weskao.com/blog/course-mechanics-canvas (search only). On Deck's format: not researched; UNVERIFIED.

## 5. Concrete next steps

### 5.1 Implementation intentions (Gollwitzer)
**Idea.** If-then plans ("When situation X arises, I will do Y") tie a cue to an action. A 2006 meta-analysis of 94 studies found a medium-to-large benefit over goal intentions alone (d = 0.65). A newer, larger synthesis of 642 tests finds smaller effects (about d = 0.27 for behavior, and lower after bias corrections), so expect a modest effect, not a large one.
**In Meet.** The session's last written step uses one fixed form: "When I next sit down to build (day, time, place), I will (one physical action with my agent), and I will know it worked when (something I can see on my device)."
**Citation.** Peter M. Gollwitzer, "Implementation Intentions: Strong Effects of Simple Plans," *American Psychologist* 54(7), 493–503, 1999, https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/99_Gollwitzer_Implementation_Intentions.pdf (search only). Peter M. Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran, "Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-analysis of Effects and Processes," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38, 69–119, 2006 (search only). Sheeran, Listrom, and Gollwitzer, meta-analysis of 642 tests (search only; the year, 2024 or 2025, is UNVERIFIED).

### 5.2 Next action (GTD)
**Idea.** David Allen defines a next action as the next "physical, visible activity" that moves a project forward. "Think about" is not an action, and you cannot "do" a project, only its next action.
**In Meet.** A plan reading "work on onboarding" is sent back by its partner. "Ask my agent to make the empty home screen show one example item, then open it on my phone" is accepted.
**Citation.** David Allen, *Getting Things Done*, Viking, 2001; rev. ed. Penguin, 2015 (search only; the definition is confirmed through several secondary quotations, and I did not open gettingthingsdone.com).

### 5.3 Definition of done and demo-driven work
**Idea.** Scrum's Definition of Done makes "finished" observable. For these cohorts, "done" is something the owner has seen work on a real device, which fits the method's rule that the agent is never the tester.
**In Meet.** Every plan ends with "I will know it worked when…", and the next session's review shows exactly that on a device.
**Citation.** *The Scrum Guide* 2020 (opened). A named "demo-driven development" source was not found; that term is UNVERIFIED.

### 5.4 Public commitment and progress monitoring: what the evidence says
**Idea.** Two results pull in different directions.
- **For:** monitoring progress improves goal attainment (138 studies, d = 0.40), and the effect is larger when progress is *reported to others or made public* and when it is *physically recorded*.
- **Against:** announcing an *identity* goal ("I'm going to be someone who…"), and having someone acknowledge it, can create a premature sense of completion and reduce the effort that follows.
- **The reconciliation:** make the specific, behavioral plan and the reported *result* public, not an identity claim or a goal statement that earns praise.

**In Meet.** Plans are posted in the panel and read aloud in 20 seconds without applause. The next session opens by checking each plan against what happened ("did / didn't / what I learned"), which is public progress monitoring.
**Citation.** Benjamin Harkin et al., "Does Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence," *Psychological Bulletin* 142(2), 198–229, 2016, https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/ (search only). Peter M. Gollwitzer, Paschal Sheeran, Verena Michalski, and Andrea E. Seifert, "When Intentions Go Public: Does Social Reality Widen the Intention-Behavior Gap?", *Psychological Science* 20(5), 612–618, 2009, https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/09_Gollwitzer_Sheeran_Seifert_Michalski_When_Intentions_.pdf (search only). Hollenbeck, Williams, and Klein, "An empirical examination of the antecedents of commitment to difficult goals," *Journal of Applied Psychology* 74, 18–23, 1989 (search only; its specific finding on publicness is UNVERIFIED).

## Recommended repeating 60-minute session: "Show, Try, Tune, Plan"

The routine is the same every week. Only the apps and the builders' own questions change. Before each session, each builder posts in the panel (as pre-work): their live app link, one thing to try, and one focusing question about their app (an open question, about something they can change themselves; NSRF Consultancy).

| Min | Segment | Leader | Each learner | Main stage shows |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | **Check-in** (RC check-ins, Daily Scrum) | Opens on time and reads out one theme from last week's exit question (Brookfield CIQ). | Posts in the panel: "What changed in my app since last week," plus the link. | Grid of everyone's app tiles with their one-line change. |
| 5–12 | **Last week's plan** (Harkin: public progress reporting; Scrum review) | Calls names in a fixed rotation (warm call); keeps each to 45 seconds. | Says: my plan was ___; on my device I saw ___; did / didn't / what I learned. | Each person's plan from last week next to their reply. |
| 12–17 | **One real thing** (replaces the generic claim) | Shows one artifact from a learner's real week, chosen with their permission: a prompt and the agent's answer, a bug found on a phone, a tester's note. Names the method step it shows, in one sentence. | Watches; writes one question about it (QFT rules: questions only, no answers). | The artifact itself, a screenshot or recording, with no slide text over it. |
| 17–37 | **Try and Tune in trios** (gallery walk, Tuning, CRP, Troika; breakouts of 2–3) | Opens rooms, rotating trios weekly (altMBA); visits each room; keeps time. | Six minutes per maker, in a fixed order. **Maker (1 min):** reads their focusing question and "try this." **Testers (2 min):** use the app on their own device, silently. **Testers (2 min):** say what they did and what happened on the screen, then "I wonder if…" (warm, then cool); the maker is muted and takes notes. **Maker (1 min):** "What I'm taking away." | Timer and the step for each room (Stachowiak: instructions on screen). |
| 37–45 | **What we saw** (What / So What) | Asks each trio for one observation from use, then asks "what does that suggest?" | One person per trio reports a fact first, meaning second. | The observations collected as they are said. |
| 45–55 | **Next build plan** (Gollwitzer if-then, GTD next action, Definition of Done) | Shows the fixed form; gives 3 silent minutes; then reads plans in rotation. Sends back any plan that is not a physical action. | Writes: "When I next sit down to build (when/where), I will (one action with my agent), and I'll know it worked when (what I'll see on my device). Who will try it: ___." Reads it in 20 seconds. No applause. | Everyone's plans, which become next week's "Last week's plan" screen. |

**Variant for weeks 3 to 5.** Replace 17–37 with a featured round for two builders, 10 minutes each, using the compressed Tuning Protocol or CRP in plenary. ## Five rules for writing questions shown to the group

1. **Ask what someone did or saw before asking what it means or what to do.** "What happened on your screen when you tapped Save?" comes before "What should change?" *Source:* Liberating Structures, What / So What / Now What (facts, then meaning, then action); Lerman, CRP step 1 (statements of meaning come from experiencing the work).
2. **If it tells people to do something, label it a task, not a question.** "Write your next prompt" is an instruction: show it as "Now: write your next build plan (3 minutes)," with a form. A question has more than one reasonable answer and invites thinking. *Source:* Right Question Institute QFT (statements and questions are kept distinct); Brookfield and Preskill (discussion needs genuine questions, not "any questions?").
3. **Make it open and neutral, with no opinion or right answer built in.** Not "Did the onboarding confuse you?" but "What did you do first when the app opened?" *Source:* Lerman, CRP step 3 ("a question is neutral when it contains no opinion"); NSRF Consultancy (avoid yes/no questions).
4. **Let the builder's own question lead.** The group's questions serve the maker's focusing question about their own app, a question they can act on themselves; the leader does not write one question for everyone. *Source:* Tuning Protocol (focusing question posted for all to see); NSRF Consultancy ("If your answer is not you, you need to change your focus"); CRP step 2 (maker as questioner).
5. **One question at a time, with writing time and named turns.** Show the question, give 30 to 60 seconds of silent writing in the panel, then warm-call names in rotation; wait again after each answer. *Source:* Rowe, 1974 and 1986 (wait time); Liberating Structures 1-2-4-All (silent individual minute first); Brookfield, Circle of Voices; Dallimore et al., 2013 (structured calling raised voluntary participation).
