**Where the cohort is.** Everyone has kept a noticing log for five days,
one line whenever one of its four prompts happened: "I wished something
worked differently," "I gave up on something I care about," "I did
something the long way because nothing helped," or "someone I know
struggled with the same thing." Nobody has chosen a problem, and nobody
has an idea for an app yet. That is on purpose, because none of my apps
began as an idea for an app. Each began with part of my life that kept
bothering me, and I wrote down why it mattered and looked around before
I built anything ([`01-mining-bens-process.md`](../research/curriculum/01-mining-bens-process.md),
phases 1 and 2).

Tonight turns those logs into one problem each, heard by the group,
held to real tests, and turned into two conversations that everyone
plans before they leave. The week after tonight is
[stage 00](../path/00-why-we-build.md) and [stage
01](../path/01-first-prototype.md): the problem tested in two real
conversations, and the smallest real version of the app in the hands of
one of those people.

**Find the problem before the app.**

## The move and the question

**Write.** This week leads with writing, from a line in the log to a
problem statement to the questions for two conversations, with the
group's questions in between, and the first prompt written once the
problem has held up.

The question the whole week turns on, the driving question for the
project: **What problem in your own life is worth building something
for, and who else has it?** It comes straight from the first principle,
which asks that the builder has "written down what the problem is, who
has it, and why a computer cannot simply solve it for them"
([`PRINCIPLES.md`](../human-shaped/PRINCIPLES.md), principle 1).

**What to watch for tonight.**

- A log line that is already an app idea ("an app that..."). Steer it
  back to what happened: "When did you last wish for that, and what
  were you doing?"
- A problem that is really a measurement or a ranking, with nobody's
  story in it. Ask about it with a question, not a verdict: "Who is in
  this, and what happens to them?"
- A problem statement that names a technology ("an app that uses AI
  to...") instead of a situation in someone's life.
- Questions in Hear each problem that drift from what happened toward
  what the app should do, or that turn into advice.
- Anyone who arrives without a log. Give them the four prompts and five
  minutes to write from memory, since most people can remember a week.

## What your log holds

Open by saying what you read in Prep's answers and what you changed
because of it, and then ask everyone to keep their log open for the
whole hour. In a fixed order, each person reads one line from their five
days of noticing and says who else it happens to, in about thirty
seconds. Nobody shows the whole log or is asked to, and each person
decides which lines leave it. Say before the first turn that app ideas
wait, so that a line about what happened is the only kind anyone reads.

Listen for lines that come back more than once across the five days,
because a thing that keeps happening is closer to my definition
("something that has gone unsolved for months or even years") than a
thing that happened once, and for lines about someone else, because
human-shaped problems are rarely one person's alone. A log that is a
list of app ideas, or one that says only "nothing happened," is not a
problem tonight: the first is the habit the log is meant to break, and
the second is what the four prompts are for.

## Hear each problem

Each builder gets about five minutes, in the same order as before. They
read the log line they keep coming back to and say why, then each person
asks one question about what happened (when, who was there, what they
tried), never about an app, and the builder ends by saying which problem
they will keep and who else has it. The builder answers each question
briefly and writes the others down. In the 75-minute version this runs
in groups of three, two minutes to read the line, five for the
questions, and four to say which problem they will keep, and you visit
each room, saying so as you arrive so that nobody stops to perform.

This is where the difference between a problem a computer can take
control of and one that belongs to people does its work, said about the
problem in front of you rather than as a talk. My four computer-shaped
problems start with the verbs measure, find the best price, categorize,
and map
([`computer-shaped-problems.md`](../human-shaped/computer-shaped-problems.md#computer-shaped-and-human-shaped)),
so when a builder's problem turns into one of those, ask who is in it
and what happens to them, and let the group's next question be about
that person. Watch, too, for a problem that is really a solution, and
ask what happened the last time instead.

## Hold yours to the tests

Alone, with the agent closed, each person writes the problem they kept
in the form of my published examples: what happens in their life, in
the first person, then "I need something that..." and what a better
life would look like, without naming a technology
([`problem-statements.md`](../human-shaped/problem-statements.md)).
Then they put it on the design stage, from the *Problem tests*
template, and hold it to the ten tests from stage 00, marking each yes,
not yet, or I don't know:

1. Solving it would make my life better, happier, or more resonant.
2. I wonder and question about it often.
3. It has gone unsolved for months or even years.
4. I cannot solve it myself with the tools and resources I have now.
5. Real people have it, and I can name them.
6. It is not mine alone. Other people would know it when they heard it.
7. A computer cannot simply solve it for me.
8. It is about people's stories, not about data points.
9. Solving it would not make a new problem for someone else.
10. At least one person would be glad of even a first version.

Everyone reads one another's boards. Say out loud that a "not yet" or
an "I don't know" is the next thing to find out rather than a failure,
and that most of them are answered by talking to people, which is what
the week is for. Nobody's problem passes or fails tonight. When you walk
the board, tests 7 and 8 are where a computer-shaped problem shows
itself, so ask that builder what happens in their life instead.

## Plan your two conversations

This part happens with the agent closed, because stage 00 asks everyone
to talk to two people who might have the problem before they build
anything. Put the form on the stage:

- Who I will talk to: ___ and ___
- Tell me about the last time you ___
- What did you try? What happened next?

Each person names two real people and writes the questions they will
ask, about a specific time in the past and never about an app (Stanford
d.school's interview guidance and Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*,
both gathered in
[`06-instructional-design.md`](../research/curriculum/06-instructional-design.md)).
They write in the box in their panel during quiet minutes. In the last
two minutes each person reads their first question aloud, in the same
order, with no applause and no comment, and those names and questions
open next week.

The first prompt comes later in the week, once the problem has held up
in the conversations, and it starts with my stem: "I want to build an
app that helps ___ to ___." It asks for the smallest real version, one
platform, live at its own address, with real data, in the hands of one
of those two people, and it carries one "It must never" line. Mine came
from Archive Watch: "I don't want AI making lists and writing copy. Any
time we can use metadata or user copy/categorization from archive.org."
Nobody has to agree with it, but every app in the cohort will meet a
choice like it, and the agent will reach for whatever is cheapest
unless someone wrote down otherwise.

## Your questions

One private question in the panel: "What questions do you still have?"
Only the leaders read the answers. Answer two or three aloud, the rest
before week 2, and open week 2 by saying what you read.

The week's own question, "How sure are you, right now, that someone
besides you has this problem?" (a scale of 5, from "Not sure at all" to
"I could name them"), goes on the session page before week 2, beside
each builder's own question. It is not a score: it tells you who went
into the conversations with names, and the people at the low end are
the ones to write to first.

## Between tonight and next week

This is the rest of the week's round, done on their own and with their
agent:

1. **Have the two conversations.** Ask about a specific time in the
   past, never what someone usually does or would do, listen more than
   you talk, and never describe an app. A good first question starts
   "Tell me about the last time you..." and then stops. Keep the notes
   private unless you choose to share them.
2. **Revise the problem statement** with what you heard, and write who
   has it and why a computer cannot simply solve it for them.
3. **Write the why with your agent,** in
   [stage 00](../path/00-why-we-build.md), until the "Why we build"
   paragraph in `AGENTS.md` sounds like you, with the line from "It must
   never" written there and dated.
4. **Publish** a short post to the cohort: your problem, who has it, and
   why a computer cannot simply solve it.
5. **Keep building the smallest real version,** in
   [stage 01](../path/01-first-prototype.md), from the prompt you sent
   tonight, and put it in the hands of one of the two people you talked
   to.

## The task, and its evidence

**The task.** Turn a line from your log into one problem statement,
hold it to the tests, have two conversations with people who might
share the problem, revise it with what you heard, and then send your
first prompt. Then write the why, publish your problem, and put the smallest
real version of the app in the hands of one of those two people.

**Evidence a person can open.** The problem statement, first version
and revised, kept in the app's note and the repository. The first
prompt, as sent. Notes from two conversations, each about a specific
time in the past, which stay with the student unless they choose to
share them. The "Why we build" paragraph in `AGENTS.md`, with one line
the app will not cross, dated and in the student's words. The published
post. Together these are the first principle's own evidence
([`PRINCIPLES.md`](../human-shaped/PRINCIPLES.md), principle 1, "How
you can tell").

**On the credential.** Recognitions this week can name "Asked for
feedback and used it," for a statement revised after the two
conversations; "Helped a classmate see their work more clearly," for a
question that turned someone's log line back toward what happened; and
"Wrote it in their own voice," for a problem statement nobody could
mistake for anyone else's. Students do the thing itself, and what they
make is the evidence; nobody writes a report about it
([`05-inquiry-and-evidence.md`](../research/curriculum/05-inquiry-and-evidence.md)).

## When someone is stuck

Every entry in the [stuck library](../stuck/README.md) starts with a
question to ask yourself, then what to try, then who to ask. This week,
point people to:

- [Stuck writing](../stuck/writing/README.md), for when no line in the
  log looks like a problem, the statement will not come, or the
  conversations feel too awkward to ask for.
- [Stuck publishing](../stuck/publishing/README.md), for when sharing
  the problem with the cohort feels too exposed.

And the ladder for any of them: name the kind of stuck, write three
lines (what you tried, what you expected, what you saw), look at the
last thing that worked, then your group, then the teacher. This week
the first rung is usually the log itself, read again from the start.

## After the session

Read the answers and write what you will say about them at the start of
week 2. Reach out within two days to anyone who missed the session,
came without a log, or did not name their two people, and offer to help
them find their two people.

Next week, everyone brings back a problem that has met real people,
and the first version of the app, live at its address.
