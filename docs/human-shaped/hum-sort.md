Eight problems, all written by me, in no particular order. Some are
human-shaped and some are computer-shaped. Sort them before you read
anyone's definition of the difference, including mine, because the point
is to notice what you already feel.

1. I want to measure the amount of negative sentiment online for any
   given public figure.
2. I do not have time to read books in my life. I need something that
   fits my way of existing that will still let me enjoy great novels and
   non-fiction every day.
3. I want to categorize every conversation I have and chronicle them as a
   way of measuring my impact on others.
4. I lack inspiration for what to do with my child to entertain them
   without screens. I need something that will constantly suggest new
   things to do that cater to both of our interests.
5. I want to map socioeconomic data by neighborhood so that I can use it
   to choose the optimal route for Halloween trick or treating to ensure
   high-end candy.
6. I read a lot of news and "takes" from people online and I even see
   newsworthy things out in the real world, and I would love a way to
   chronicle it all into a cohesive narrative, an understanding of how I
   see things to remind myself why I believe what I believe.
7. I want to find the best price for my grocery list on delivery apps so
   that I can comparison shop and combine trips for the cheapest (and
   least convenient) trips for the delivery drivers.
8. I want an easy way to align my political beliefs with who and what
   I'm voting for when I get my ballot or vote in person.

## How to sort

1. **Write.** Put each number on one of two sides, in your note or on
   the cohort's board. Do not name the sides yet. Beside each one, write
   a few words on why it went where it did.
2. **Play.** With your group, compare your two piles. Find the one you
   disagree about most, and talk about it until you can each say what
   the other person saw.
3. **Publish.** Now name the two sides, out loud or on the board, in
   your own words. Then read my names for them below.

## My two sides

Problems 2, 4, 6, and 8 are human-shaped, and 1, 3, 5, and 7 are
computer-shaped. The longer page,
[computer-shaped problems](computer-shaped-problems.md#computer-shaped-and-human-shaped),
has my definition and the same examples, labeled. Its closing lines are
the difference in one breath:

> Human-shaped problems see the world as interrelated stories to be
> told, experienced, and added to.
>
> Computer-shaped problems see the world as disparate data points to be
> aggregated, quantified, and leveraged.

If your sides came out differently from mine, that is worth more than
agreeing with me. Write down which one, and why, and bring it back.

**Go deeper.** Evgeny Morozov has a name for the computer-shaped habit,
*solutionism*: recasting complex human situations as problems with neat,
computable answers. Read
[his interview with Public Books](https://www.publicbooks.org/the-folly-of-technological-solutionism-an-interview-with-evgeny-morozov/)
after you have sorted, not before, so the word names something you have
already felt. (The sorting activity itself comes from the curriculum
research, [02, idea 2](../research/curriculum/02-landscape.md).)
