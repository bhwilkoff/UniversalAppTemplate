Start something

A day of building where learning counts.

A Human-Shaped Hackathon is a day, or half of one, in which every person builds one real app of their own for people they can name, and what they learned while building it is what counts as success. This kit has the schedule for both lengths, the words to open the day, the show-and-tell that closes it, a checklist for the organizer, and the sheets to print.

What makes it a Human-Shaped Hackathon.

Anyone can run one of these without asking anyone's permission, and change almost everything about it to fit their own room. These five things are what make it human-shaped, so keep them, and if you keep them, you can call it a Human-Shaped Hackathon.

  1. Learning is what counts as success, so an app that only half works when the day is over can still be a success, as long as its builder can say what they learned.
  2. Each person builds one real app of their own, for people they can name, in their own GitHub account, where it stays theirs after the day is over.
  3. The four questions are on the wall all day: does it deepen understanding, does it invite participation, does it support human agency, and is it clear rather than clever?
  4. The day ends with a show-and-tell, in which each person shows what they built and one decision their values changed.
  5. Nobody wins a prize for speed or polish, for reasons below.

Who comes, and what they bring.

Anyone who has a problem worth building for is welcome, including people who have never built an app, because the method is written for them. Ask everyone to bring a computer, their phone, a GitHub account, and an AI agent they can use (Getting set up shows the cheapest ways that work), and to bring a problem rather than a solution: a person or a group they know, and something in their lives that software could make easier without taking the human part away.

Setup happens before the day, never on the morning of it, because an hour lost to installing tools is an hour nobody gets back. Send the setup steps a week ahead, and still open the doors half an hour early with a table where helpers sit with anyone who is not ready yet. Plan on one helper for every eight to ten people. Helpers do not need to be experts, only people who have built something with an agent before and are glad to sit beside someone who has not.

Each person builds their own app, and people sit in threes, so that everyone has two people to show their work to through the day. Friends who want to sit together can, but each of them still builds their own.

The day, from 9:30 to 4:30.

Change the clock to suit your room, and keep the order and the lengths, since the breaks and the looking back are where most of the learning happens.

  1. 30 min9:30

    Doors and setup

    Hand out name tags and coffee, and open the setup table. Anyone whose agent cannot yet open their own repository gets a helper first, so that nobody starts the day behind.

  2. 15 min10:00

    The opening

    Give the opening words, point to the four questions on the wall, and hand out the sticky notes and the show-and-tell cards.

  3. 45 min10:15

    Write

    Before choosing anything, each person writes three ideas, each one a problem for people they can name, and then picks the one whose people they can see most clearly, because a room in a hurry settles on its first idea. Then everyone starts stage 00: why the app exists, who it is for, and one line it will never cross, all written down before the agent writes any code, and then tested against the four questions.

  4. 15 min11:00

    Read it to two people

    In threes, each person reads their "why" aloud, and the other two ask the four questions back.

  5. 45 min11:15

    The first build

    Everyone builds toward stage 01: one platform, with real data, open on the builder's own phone. Helpers go to the stuck sticky notes first.

  6. 15 min12:00

    Look back

    In threes, each person says what they learned, one thing the agent got wrong, and what they will ask it for next.

  7. 45 min12:15

    Lunch

    Eat away from the screens if the room allows it, and nobody has to talk about their app.

  8. 60 min1:00

    The second build

    Keep going toward the app working on the phone, and ask the agent why it did what it did as often as you ask it to do something.

  9. 20 min2:00

    Show it to your three

    Each person shows the app on their phone and explains one decision in it with the agent closed, and the other two ask one question each, of which the best is usually "what did you give up?"

  10. 10 min2:20

    A break

  11. 60 min2:30

    The last build

    Stop adding things and finish the one thing you want to show. In the last fifteen minutes, everyone fills in the front of their show-and-tell card.

  12. 45 min3:30

    The show-and-tell

    Three minutes each, as described below.

  13. 15 min4:15

    The closing circle

    Each person answers three questions in a sentence or two: what did you learn, what surprised you, and what will you do differently? Then give the closing words.

The half day, from 1:00 to 5:00.

Four hours is enough for one real round of building, as long as everyone arrives already set up, so for a half day the setup steps are not optional, and an online setup hour in the week before is worth offering. People write two ideas instead of three, and the trio check-in folds into the look back.

  1. 15 min1:00

    The opening

    The opening words, the four questions, the sticky notes, and the cards.

  2. 30 min1:15

    Write

    Two ideas, then one, then why it exists, who it is for, and the line it will never cross, tested against the four questions.

  3. 45 min1:45

    The first build

    Toward one platform, with real data, on the builder's own phone.

  4. 10 min2:30

    Look back

    In threes: what you learned, what the agent got wrong, and what you will ask for next.

  5. 10 min2:40

    A break

  6. 60 min2:50

    The last build

    Finish the one thing you want to show, and fill in the card in the last ten minutes.

  7. 50 min3:50

    The show-and-tell

    Three minutes each, for up to about twelve people; with more, split into two rooms.

  8. 20 min4:40

    The closing circle

    What you learned, what surprised you, and what you will do differently, then the closing words.

The words for the opening and the close.

Say them your own way. The opening tells people how the day ends before it starts, because the ending is what makes this hackathon different from the ones most people have been to, and it is also where the room hears, out loud, how we treat one another.

The opening, about three minutes

Good morning, and thank you for giving a whole day to this. I want to tell you how today ends before it starts, because it is different from most hackathons. This afternoon, each of you will show the room what you built, on your own phone, and tell us one decision your values changed along the way. Nobody is judging and there are no prizes, and an app that only half works is welcome at the show-and-tell, because what counts today is what you learned while building it.

Everything you make today is yours, in your own GitHub account, and you can keep building it tomorrow or let it go. The four questions on the wall are the only rules for what you build: does it deepen understanding, does it invite participation, does it support human agency, and is it clear rather than clever?

Here is how we treat one another today, said out loud rather than signed. Let people finish their thought, nobody acts surprised that someone else has not heard of something, and nobody takes over someone else's keyboard, even to help. If something lands badly, say so kindly and we will keep going. When you are stuck, put the (color) sticky note on the lid of your laptop, and a helper will come to you.

The close, about a minute

Thank you for showing us what you made, and especially for showing us the parts that did not work yet, which is where most of today's learning was. What you built is yours to keep going with, and there are three places to take it from here: the humanshaped discussions on GitHub, where you can show it to people who build this way; a free five-week cohort, if you want to take it all the way to an app people can install; or the template, if you want to keep going on your own. I will post what we made and what we learned, with your permission, so that the next room can start from it.

The show-and-tell, and one decision your values changed.

Each person has three minutes. They open the app on their own phone (on the screen if the room has a way to show a phone, or passed around if it does not), say who it is for, show it doing one real thing, and then tell the room one decision their values changed: what they wanted to build, what one of their values said about it, and what they did instead. That last part is the heart of the day, because a value that never changed a decision is decoration.

An app that does not work yet is shown the same way, with what happened and what its builder learned from it, and that counts. The room asks one question at most, and everyone is thanked the same way. Afterward, two people write on the back of each builder's card one thing that worked and one that did not, so that everyone leaves with feedback in someone else's handwriting.

Why there are no prizes for speed or polish.

Most hackathons end with judges and prizes, and that format is good at what it was built for, which is finding the most promising new product in a room. This day is built for something else. Research on how hackathons are planned finds that competition suits events meant to produce new products, while events meant for learning, for meeting people, or for welcoming newcomers work better with cooperation and small prizes or none at all.

There is a second reason, which is particular to building with an AI agent. An agent can make something look finished in minutes, so a prize for polish rewards the agent's work rather than the builder's, and a prize for speed rewards skipping exactly the parts of the day that are about thinking: writing down why, asking the four questions, and looking back. The person who learned the most is often the one whose app only half works by the afternoon, and no judge would pick them. Ranking people in front of one another also tends to lower the motivation of everyone who did not win, which is most of the room.

The cost is real, since some people come to hackathons for the contest, and this one will not be for them. What everyone gets instead is the show-and-tell, written feedback from two people, and, if they want it, help before they leave with adding a declaration and the working toward mark to their repository.

The organizer's checklist.

Before

  • Decide what you want people to leave with, before you plan anything else, and put it at the top of the announcement.
  • Find a room with a step-free entrance, an accessible restroom, a power outlet within reach of every seat (or enough power strips), wifi that can carry everyone at once, and a quiet corner.
  • Find one helper for every eight to ten people.
  • Send the announcement at least three weeks ahead.
  • A week ahead, send the setup steps, ask people to reply when their agent can open their own repository, and offer a short online setup hour for anyone who cannot.
  • Print the four questions and the principles for the wall, the day's sheet, and a show-and-tell card for each person, with spares.
  • Buy sticky notes in two colors, and arrange food, labeled with what is in it.

On the day

  • Arrive an hour early, test the wifi on your own laptop and phone, and put the sheets on the wall where every seat can see them.
  • Run the setup table until the opening, so that nobody starts building until they can talk to their agent in their own repository.
  • Give the opening words, and keep time for every part, with a five-minute warning before each change.
  • Send helpers to the stuck color first, and ask them to say when they arrive, so that nobody has to perform for them.
  • Keep the breaks and lunch, even when people would rather keep building.
  • Run the show-and-tell in the order people signed up, three minutes each, with one question at most.
  • Close with the circle and the closing words, and end on time.

After

  • Within a week, post in the humanshaped discussions: where and when, how many came, what people built (linking only the apps whose builders said yes), what the closing circle learned, and one thing to change next time.
  • Send everyone a short note with those links, the discussions, the cohorts, and the template.
  • Remind builders that an app made from the template is considered for the directory without anyone having to ask.
  • Say in the same discussion what in this kit did not work, so that it gets better for the next person.

The announcement, ready to adapt.

Change everything in square brackets, and keep the line about access, even if it only says what the room does not have yet.

Human-Shaped Hackathon: a day of building software for people you know

[Day and date], [start time] to [end time], at [the place, the room, and how to find it].

Bring a problem rather than a solution: a person or a group you know, and something in their lives that an app could make easier without taking the human part away. You will spend the day building one real app of your own for them with an AI agent, using the free method at humanshaped.org, and before everyone goes home, each person shows what they made. You do not need to have built an app before.

There are no judges and no prizes. What counts is what you learn, and everything you build stays yours.

Bring a laptop, your phone, a GitHub account, and an AI agent you can use. A week before, we will send you the setup steps, and helpers will be there early on the day for anyone who needs a hand.

Access: [a step-free entrance, an accessible restroom, power at every seat, a quiet space, and food labeled with what is in it]. If something else would help you take part, tell [your name] at [how to reach you] and we will do what we can.

It is free. Say you are coming at [link or email].
