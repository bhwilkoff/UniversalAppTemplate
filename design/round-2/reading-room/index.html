Software built around people, rather than over them.

I have spent this year building apps with an AI agent, and what I keep coming back to is how much of a life cannot be handed to a computer. Human Shaped is where we learn to build software for those parts together, in free five-week cohorts, starting from an idea you already have.

The idea

Some problems were never computer-shaped.

In March, I typed a page about a post I had read on Bluesky, which said that we are only looking to solve "computer-shaped problems." A computer-shaped problem is one you can hand to a machine and be done with. But, the things that matter most to most of us (learning, community, the people we love) only happen when a person does the work, and software that pretends otherwise quietly takes that work away.

Human-shaped software takes the mechanical work off people's hands and leaves them the learning. Here is the difference in one of my own apps:

  • Computer-shaped

    A model writes a blurb for each of ten thousand films in an afternoon, and most folks would never know.

  • Human-shaped

    Archive Watch pulls each film's notes from the people who uploaded and reviewed it on archive.org, which is slower, and the words stay theirs.

The phrase comes from a post on Bluesky that started all of this, and I am grateful for it.

The method

Write it down, prove it, live with it.

An agent will happily write most of your code, and yet there are three things it cannot do for you. Every round of building makes the same three moves, whether it is your first week or your fortieth, because they are where you actually learn.

  1. Write it down

    Writing is how you find out what you believe, and a value you have not written down is one the agent building your app has no way to keep.

  2. Prove it

    Proving is how you find out whether it is true, so every "it works" points at something a person can look at on a real device, rather than the agent's word for it.

  3. Live with it

    Living with the app is how you find out what you missed, because the values you could not have predicted only show up when you use it every day, with the people it is for.

Cohorts

Five weeks, with other people, for free.

A cohort runs for about five weeks, with one live session each week on Google Meet, and every person in it builds one real app of their own for people they can actually name. You will be able to see one another's apps as they grow, talk them through on GitHub, and bring something back to show at the end of every week.

Every session is recorded, so missing a week never means falling behind. I want cohorts to stay free, and in time the people who finish one will be able to lead the next.

Before
Your own app's address, open on your phone.
Week 1
Why the app exists, the people outside your screen, one rule in your own words, and the app running on your phone.
Week 2
One thing your app does, shown on two platforms, each in its own idiom.
Week 3
A screenshot that proved a fix, and your app's own look beside the template's.
Week 4
Your app on someone else's device, and what that person said about it.
Week 5
A feature you wanted from your own use, and one value with the decision it changed, shown to everyone (guests welcome).

The first cohort's dates will be posted here, and signing in is how you will hear about them first.

Built this way

Four apps came before the method.

Before I asked anyone to build like this, I built four apps this way myself, and the template you will start from is what they taught me. In a cohort, your app gets a page like these, read straight from your repository with your permission, so your classmates can follow it as it grows.

  • Archive Watch

    "A cinematheque for the Internet Archive. Free, public domain, no account."

    Started April 17, 2026, and now on Apple TV, iPhone, iPad, Mac, Android, Google TV, Fire TV, Fire tablets, Roku and the web, with 4,306 commits by the end of September.

    It gave the template most of what it knows about shared data, TV platforms and testing on real devices, and the rule that only essential words belong on screen.

  • Tidbits Trivia

    "Trivia from the whole of Wikipedia. Play solo, keep a daily streak, or pass the phone."

    First built June 16, 2026, and now on the web, Apple's platforms, Android, and Windows through the Microsoft Store.

    It taught the template how to play together across platforms, and the rule that a computer opponent is always labeled as one.

  • Bsky Dreams

    "Built for people who want to think more, not less."

    A way to read Bluesky, on the web and the iPhone.

    It gave the template a feed ranked by the reader's own values rather than by engagement, and a reader mode for the links people share.

  • BOBA Playbook

    "The definitive companion app for the Bo Jackson Battle Arena trading card game."

    On the web and Android.

    It is where the four questions every feature has to answer came from, and it stands as the proof that this method holds for large databases, huge numbers of files, camera scanning, 3D on iOS and pricing.

    No longer in active development.
