1. Did the antibodies protect them?

All the crew tested negative before leaving port, but the virus got on board anyway. By the time the ship came back, 103 of the 117 crew without antibodies were infected. None of the 3 with antibodies were.

Maybe the antibodies protected those 3. Or maybe they were just lucky. To decide, we ask one question all the way through:

If antibodies did nothing at all, how often would those 3 particular people all escape, just by luck?
If the answer is “often”, luck explains it. If it is “almost never”, luck is a poor explanation.

Two ways to describe “just luck”

(a) The hat model

Put all 120 names in a hat. Pull out 103 names, without putting any back. Those 103 people get sick.

Exactly 103 sick, every time. Only who gets sick is luck.

(b) The coin model

Each person flips their own weighted coin. It lands “sick” with chance 103/120 (about 86%).

Who gets sick and how many are both luck: usually somewhere around 95 to 111.

In both models the 3 antibody holders are treated exactly like everyone else. That is what “antibodies did nothing” means. Statisticians call this assumption the null hypothesis.

    3. Warm-up: a small hat

    Before the ship, a tiny version. 10 people, including two we care about, A and B. The hat model pulls out 3 names; those 3 get sick.

    What's the chance that A and B are both spared? Follow one lucky path: every name pulled is someone other than A or B.

    1. Press Pull the next name. Read the fraction that appears: the chance that this name is not A or B.
    2. Do it three times. Watch how the top and bottom numbers change.
    3. To stay lucky every time, multiply the three fractions.

    Why is the second fraction 7/9, not 8/10?

    One name is already out of the hat, so 9 names are left, and 7 of them are not A or B.
    A or B might already be sick, so we count one fewer safe person.
    The chance of getting sick falls as the virus spreads.

    4. The ship, by multiplying fractions in R

    Same idea, bigger hat: 120 names, 103 pulled out, and we want the 3 antibody holders never pulled.

    6. The coin model, by the multiplication rule

    Now model (b): each person flips their own weighted coin, “sick” with chance 103/120. One person's coin doesn't affect anyone else's. That makes the 3 people independent.

    • Chance one person is spared: 1 − 103/120 = 17/120, about 0.142.
    • From the pre-class reading, for independent events: P(A and B and C) = P(A) × P(B) × P(C).
    • So the chance all 3 are spared is p_spare × p_spare × p_spare. In R, x^2 means x × x, and x^3 means x × x × x.

    Real infections aren't independent: a sick bunkmate makes you more likely to get sick. The coin model ignores that to keep things simple.

    8. Where else could it be luck?

How would you set up “just luck” for it as a hat or a coin model, with which three numbers? If luck turned out to be a poor explanation, what would you check before crediting the reason you suspect?

    Points to compare
    • A usable luck model names three numbers, like 120 crew, 103 sick and 3 antibody holders in step 2: how many people in all, how many had the outcome, and how many are in the small group.
    • The hat fits when the total with the outcome is fixed (steps 2, 5); the coins fit when each person’s chance is the same and independent of everyone else’s (step 6). For a small group drawn from a big pool, the two give close answers (the table in step 7).
    • A tiny chance makes luck a poor explanation, not an impossible one, and it leaves other explanations open. Check how the small group differs from the rest: a study group that chose its own members, or antibody holders who slept or worked apart from the crew (step 7).

    You should now be able to:

    • state a “just luck” assumption (a null hypothesis) as a model you can calculate with (step 2);
    • find a probability by multiplying a chain of fractions (steps 3, 4) or, for independent events, with the multiplication rule (step 6);
    • check a calculation by simulation, and expect the simulation to land close, not exactly (step 5);
    • judge from a small chance that luck is a poor explanation, while other explanations stay open (step 7).

    Assumptions
    • dataFrom the course workbook's excerpt of a news report on Bloom and colleagues' study: 120 crew tested before and after; 3 had neutralizing antibodies before the trip and none of them were infected; 103 of the other 117 were.
    • simplificationBoth models assume antibodies did nothing (the null hypothesis) and that test results are correct.
    • distributionHat model: the 103 infected are a random 103 of the 120, every set equally likely. Coin model: each person is infected independently with chance 103/120.
    • parameterSimulations use set.seed(350) and 10,000 voyages.

    Left out: p-values and formal hypothesis tests by name, and how contagion between crew members would change the answer.
