Turn the V Dial: District 13

In 1970 the Capitol imposed a policy on District 13 that was expected to affect its GDP per capita. To estimate the effect, we build a synthetic District 13: a weighted average of two untreated donors, District 12 and District 2, chosen to resemble District 13 on four pre-treatment predictors, each averaged over 1961–1969: GDP per capita, education, industrial share and unemployment. Two sets of weights are involved, found by a nested optimization.

Inner loop: given predictor weights V (how much each predictor matters), find the donor weights W* that minimize the V-weighted mismatch between District 13 and its synthetic twin on the predictors. Outer loop: choose V so that the resulting W* best reproduces District 13's actual GDP per capita before 1970 (here, in 1965 and 1969). On this page you turn the V dial by hand; the inner loop runs automatically, and the outer score updates. Search for V* runs the outer loop for you.

Targets. Each predictor, used alone, has a target: the W that makes the synthetic district match District 13 exactly on that predictor. For GDP it is 0.70: 70% District 12 and 30% District 2 gives 0.70 × 4.55 + 0.30 × 5.45 = 4.82, District 13's value. Education's target is also 0.70 and unemployment's 0.75. Industrial share's target is 1.53: District 13's share (0.48) is higher than both donors' (0.40 and 0.25), and matching it would take 153% weight on District 12 and −53% on District 2 (1.53 × 0.40 − 0.53 × 0.25 = 0.48). Weights can't be negative and must add to 100%, so W must stay between 0 and 1, and that target is out of reach: the closest any blend gets is W = 1, with an industrial share of 0.40.

A fictional example set in Panem, the world of Suzanne Collins’s The Hunger Games; all numbers are invented for teaching. The treatment is a Capitol policy imposed on District 13 in 1970 and expected to change its GDP per capita. Predictors are averages over 1961–1969; the outer score uses GDP per capita in 1965 and 1969. The design is inspired by Abadie, A. and Gardeazabal, J. (2003), “The Economic Costs of Conflict: A Case Study of the Basque Country,” American Economic Review 93(1): 112–132, doi:10.1257/000282803321455188, which built a synthetic Basque Country from other Spanish regions. None of the data here are theirs.

Why go through V at all?

The outer loop can only choose among the W*'s the inner loop produces. Fitting W straight to the outcome would do better on this score but gives up the predictor matching. Synthetic control is credible because a synthetic unit that tracks both the observed outcome path and the observed predictors is presumably also matching the unobserved factors that drive the outcome.
