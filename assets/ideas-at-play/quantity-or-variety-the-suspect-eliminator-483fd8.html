Quantity or variety?

An inductive argument can be strengthened two ways: more of the same evidence, or a different kind of evidence. Wilkins calls these enumerative and eliminative induction. You have a budget. Spend it, and watch which kind of spending actually narrows the field.

Two ways to make an inductive argument stronger

Open each one. Then watch which kind of spending each purchase below counts as.

You already have a blood analysis. You commission a second one. The new reading agrees with the first, and your belief that the culprit’s blood type really is O goes up.

What this buys you is a firmer premise. If readings are perfect it can buy nothing else, since the second reading can only repeat the first. Once readings can be wrong, agreement concentrates belief and disagreement spreads it back out — so quantity starts to pay.

In the reading: enlarging the sample. Every donkey you meet after the first is enumerative — the argument about donkeys and carrots gets stronger because the sample grows, not because a new kind of observation enters.

You have a blood analysis, and now you buy the security camera. It speaks to something the blood could not: height. Suspects who matched on blood but are the wrong height now fall away.

The name comes from what it does to competing hypotheses. Each new kind of evidence pushes down a different set of rival explanations, and the hypothesis that survives all of them is the stronger for it.

In the reading: blood type, then height, then handedness. Each is a different axis, so each cuts the field again — which is why eliminative induction matters most when you are trying to establish a cause.
