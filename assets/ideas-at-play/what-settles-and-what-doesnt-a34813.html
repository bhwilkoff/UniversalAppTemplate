3. A distribution with no mean

The Cauchy distribution looks like a bell curve with fatter tails: centred at 0, but now and then it throws out a huge value, like 5,000.

Take the running mean of Cauchy draws, as for the coin. Will it settle near 0 as the draws pile up?

Yes, like any average of many draws
Yes, but more slowly than for the Normal
No, it never settles

The Normal running means close in on 0. The Cauchy ones jump whenever one huge draw arrives, early or late, and never settle. The law of large numbers needs a population mean to converge to, and the Cauchy distribution has none: its tails are too heavy. Coins, dice and almost everything you'll measure do have one.

What the law of large numbers says

As the number of independent trials grows, the share of successes (or the average) tends to get closer and closer to the true probability (or mean).

It says nothing about the next trial, and nothing about the count catching up.

4. “I'm due”

As a group: a friend has lost six even-money bets in a row on a fair game and says, “The law of large numbers says it evens out, so I'm due a win.” What's the best reply?

What settled, and what didn't

  • Settled: the share of heads, near ½ (step 2).
  • Didn't: the gap between heads and tails, which typically grew (step 2).
  • Never moved: the chance of the next flip, ½ after any streak (step 2).
  • Didn't: the running mean of Cauchy draws, which has no mean to settle on (step 3).

5. Your own streak

As a group: think of a place in your own studies or life where people expect a run of good or bad luck to even out (a striker “due” a goal, say, but find your own). Is each try there independent of the last, and what would you look at, instead of the streak, to judge what comes next?

Points to compare
  • Independence comes first: does one try change the next (practice, tiredness, cards not put back)? If not, the streak says nothing about the next try (step 2's ten heads).
  • What settles is the long-run share, by dilution, while the count of wins minus losses typically grows (step 2). So look at a rate over many tries, not the latest run.
  • A long run can hint that the chance isn't what you assumed, like a biased wheel, but one run is weak evidence; it needs many more tries to judge.

You should now be able to:

  • explain why the share of heads settles near ½ while the gap between heads and tails grows (step 2);
  • answer “I'm due” with independence and dilution (steps 2 and 4);
  • say what the law of large numbers needs: independent tries and a mean to settle on (steps 3 and 4).

Assumptions
  • distributionFair coin: each flip is heads with probability 1/2, independently. 20 runs of 10,000 flips from a seeded generator (mulberry32); the seed is shared and changed by “New 20 runs”.
  • interpretation“Typical distance from 0.5” and “typical size” of the gap are root-mean-square values across the 20 runs. Theory: the share's SD is 0.5/√n and the gap's RMS is √n (about 3.2, 10, 32, 100). “Gap ÷ flips” is the RMS gap divided by n, which equals twice the RMS distance of the share from 0.5.
  • distributionCauchy draws are tan(π(U − ½)) with U uniform: centre 0, scale 1, no mean and no variance. Normal draws use Box–Muller.
  • simplificationCharts plot about 200 points per run on a log scale of flips; the table uses every flip.

Left out: the formal statement of the law (convergence in probability) and the central limit theorem, which explains the √n.
