The higher average income belongs to…

Definitions (Rodrik’s): a country is poor or rich if it is in the bottom or top decile of the 195 countries ranked by average income per adult, which gives 20 countries each way. “Rich in a poor country” means the top 10% of people in those 20 poor countries; “poor in a rich country” means the bottom 10% of people in those 20 rich countries. Group figures are unweighted means over the 20 countries. Default options: top and bottom 10%, income after tax and transfers, 2024, USD PPP (2025 prices).

What happened: the answer is not stable

The comparison is R = (mean over the 20 poorest countries of the top-q slice average) ÷ (mean over the 20 richest countries of the bottom-q slice average). R above 1 means rich-in-poor wins. The same 40 countries are in every cell below. R falls toward 1 and crosses it at a wide slice only after taxes and transfers, which raise the bottom of rich countries and cut their top. Assumptions and omissions
  • Parameters: 195 countries; poor and rich are the bottom and top decile by average income per adult (20 each); slices of 10%, 5% and 1%; 2024, USD PPP (2025 prices).
  • Simplification: group figures are unweighted means over the 20 countries, so a small country counts as much as a large one.
  • Simplification: the same 40 countries form the groups in every comparison, because ranking uses p0p100, which taxes do not change.
  • Data: the World Inequality Database file supplied by the author, embedded in this page.
  • Interpretation: when more than half of the 20 rich countries report $0 for the bottom slice, the ratio is withheld.
  • Interpretation: Brazil and Switzerland are an illustrative pair; neither is in the poor or rich decile group.
  • Left out: population weighting, other years, and the methods behind the WID series.
Colophon

Source: World Inequality Database (WID.world), year 2024, per adult, USD PPP at 2025 prices. Series: aptinc992j (pre-tax national income, “before tax”) and adiinc992j (post-tax disposable income, “after tax and transfers”). Countries are ranked on average income per adult (p0p100), which is identical in both series. Group averages are unweighted means of the country figures, so each country counts once. WID imputes the bottom of the distribution more heavily than the top, so bottom-slice figures are less certain than top-slice figures. Namibia’s code “NA” is kept as text.
