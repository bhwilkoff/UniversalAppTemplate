Causal inference · Regression discontinuity · Pre-class, about 15 minutes

Does turning 21 change how many people die?

Big question: what can a sudden jump at a cutoff tell us about cause, and for whom?

Before class, you will

  • Judge whether a jump at a cutoff is bigger than the month-to-month wobble in the data.
  • Explain why an RDD fits a curve on each side of the cutoff and reads the gap at the cutoff, and see how that gap depends on choices you make.
  • Separate the claims an RDD supports (a local effect at the cutoff, if nothing else jumps there) from those it does not.

Assumes you have read the start of Cunningham’s Mixtape chapter 6 (Regression Discontinuity, Bird’s Eye View of RDD). Three short parts. Your prediction, your written answers and your first choice on each question are saved, so answer for yourself before checking anything. Hover over or tab to any dotted term for its definition.

Part 1. What happens to deaths at age 21, and is it more than noise?

You are about to see the average death rate for each month of age from 19 to 23 in the United States. The cutoff is age 21, the minimum legal drinking age. The plot appears once you lock in a prediction.

Mixtape: Ch. 6 Regression Discontinuity, “Intuition of RDD” (Figure 6.3).

Before you see the data: what do you expect the death rate to do right at age 21?

It jumps up at 21
It carries on smoothly, with no break at 21
It drops at 21

You predicted a jump up. Some students predict the opposite: that nothing breaks at 21, because a birthday changes nothing about a person’s health and any higher dots are ordinary month-to-month wobble.

In one or two sentences, reply to that view, using what you see in the readout below the plot.

Resolution. A birthday does not change health, but it does change the law, and the break sits exactly at the dashed line. The 24 dots before the cutoff average 92.9, and none is more than about 2 from that average. The first dot at 21 is 104.6, 11.7 above it, far outside the usual wobble.

You predicted a smooth continuation. That is a sensible default because age changes gradually. Some students predict a jump up instead: the first dot at 21 sits well above every earlier dot, and ageing is too slow to explain a rise within a single month.

In one or two sentences, say whether that view fits the plot better than yours, using what you see in the readout below the plot.

Resolution. The jump view fits better. Smooth ageing predicts each dot near its neighbour, and the dots before 21 do behave that way: none is more than about 2 from their 92.9 average. The first dot at 21 sits 11.7 above it.

You predicted a drop. A drop is reasonable if you think legal access makes people safer, or if you notice the dots fall steadily after 21. Some students predict a jump up: the dots just right of the dashed line sit above all the dots to its left, and the later fall is a slow drift that starts from that higher level.

In one or two sentences, say whether that view fits the plot better than yours, using what you see in the readout below the plot.

Resolution. The jump view fits better. At the dashed line the rate moves from about 93 to about 105, a rise. The later decline is a gradual slope after the break, not a drop at 21.

Each dot is the average death rate for one month of age. The dashed line marks age 21.

Part 2. How big is the jump, and what does your answer depend on?

Two fitted curves now end at the dashed line, one on each side, and the thick bar between them is the estimated jump. Use the two controls: change how many months of data on each side the curves use, and whether each side is a straight line or a quadratic. Dots outside the window fade out.

Mixtape: Ch. 6 Regression Discontinuity, “Jargon and Data”: trends are how the outcome changes with the running variable, and strong trends make estimation harder.

Try at least three combinations of window and curve shape. Write down the smallest and the largest jump you found, with the settings that gave each (for example: “12 months, straight line: +9.0”). Keep them: you will need them in class.

Press “Show the naive difference”: two dashed lines mark the average of the dots in the window on each side, and the grey bar is the gap between them. Whatever window and shape you choose, the estimated jump is larger than this naive difference. Which explanation fits the plot?

What this page assumes
  • Data. Real series: Carpenter and Dobkin (2009) all-cause mortality, 48 monthly values typed by eye from the published figure, ages 19 to 23. Approximate, not microdata.
  • Simplification. The RDD fits a separate least-squares curve (straight line or quadratic) on each side of 21, using the chosen number of months on each side, and reads the gap at 21. No standard errors and no data-driven bandwidth choice.
  • Simplification. The naive difference is the average of the dots inside the window from 21 on minus the average of the dots inside the window before 21.
  • Simplification. The wobble in Part 1 is the largest distance of any dot before 21 from their average, a rough scale for noise and not a significance test.
  • Interpretation. The jump is the effect of reaching 21 for people at 21, only if everything else changes smoothly there. The page cannot test that.

Left out (covered in class): placebo outcomes, standard errors, formal bandwidth selection, density and covariate-balance tests, fuzzy designs. Adapted from “Reading the jump: regression discontinuity at age 21” by Marina Levay, CC BY 4.0.
