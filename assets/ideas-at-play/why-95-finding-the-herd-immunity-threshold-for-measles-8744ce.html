Why 95%? Finding the herd immunity threshold for measles

Use a simulated city to find where measles stops spreading.

This page assumes you know what a vaccine does. It needs only fractions and percentages. Measles lasts one to two weeks, spreads through the air and most people recover.

Here, the threshold means the vaccination coverage above which one imported case rarely starts a spreading outbreak. You will find it by simulation. R₀ is the average number of people one case infects in a population where nobody is immune.

Dials

Start from one imported case. An outbreak means 20 or more people infected, 5% of the city, so it cannot happen when fewer than 20 people can be infected. The cutoff is the lowest coverage at which fewer than 1 run in 20 is an outbreak.

Break it: imperfect vaccine, clustered neighbourhoods

First find the cutoff with 100% effectiveness and 0% clustering. Then lower effectiveness: some vaccinated people are not protected, so more people can be infected than the vaccination share suggests. Vaccinated is not the same as protected: at 90% effectiveness, 96% vaccinated protects only about 86% (0.96 × 0.90). With 97% effectiveness, 96% vaccinated is about 93% protected, below the 95% target. Then raise clustering: the unvaccinated gather in a few neighbourhoods, where a case meets mostly people who can be infected. In this 400-person city, whole neighbourhoods of 20 can fill with people who can be infected, and the 20-case cutoff is easy to reach, so the effect is exaggerated. At 100% this is an extreme stress test, not a realistic city, and not evidence that measles always breaks out at 96%. Watch the line under the grid. Does the cutoff go up or down?

DiseaseSimulated cutoff (as defined in step 2)

Herd immunity threshold. The share of people who must be immune so that one case infects, on average, fewer than one other person. Above it, chains of infection die out instead of growing. The higher R₀ is, the higher the threshold: the R₀ = 2 curve falls to almost zero at far lower coverage than the R₀ = 15 curve. The simulated cutoff is only approximate: it comes from 40 runs per point on a coarse grid in a city of 400, and chains of cases still happen by chance near the threshold. If the vaccine protects fewer than 100% of the people vaccinated, coverage must be higher: set effectiveness to 97% above and watch the cutoff.

Step 1 try-it answers. At 80% coverage: 15 × 0.20 = 3 people per case. At 90%: 15 × 0.10 = 1.5. At 97%: 15 × 0.03 = 0.45. With a vaccine that protects 97 of 100 and 95% vaccinated: 0.95 × 0.97 ≈ 0.92, so about 92% protected, not 95%.

Assumption ledger and what was left out
  • Model: agent-based, random mixing, 400 people in 20 neighbourhoods of 20, one imported case, protected people fully immune.
  • R₀ sets how many people a case infects in a city with no immunity; half of contacts are in the same neighbourhood.
  • Outbreak means 20 or more infected, so it is impossible when fewer than 20 people can be infected. The simulated cutoff is read from 40 runs per point, so it is approximate.
  • Clustering puts the unvaccinated into the first neighbourhoods; vaccine failures are placed at random. 100% clustering is an extreme stress test, exaggerated by the small city and the 20-case cutoff.
  • R₀ of 12 to 18 and about 97% two-dose protection are commonly cited figures, not estimated here. 95% is a rounded public-health target, not a value this model calculates.
  • Left out: real city contact data, age structure, travel, waning immunity, other diseases, vaccine safety debates, estimating R₀ from data, and a comparison with SIR compartment models.
