What just happened?

The current run has selection above 0, so it is not randomized. Here the estimator is biased, and the claims below about unbiasedness and about the SD do not apply to this run. Set selection to 0 to see the randomized result.

Each trial uses different students and a different random split, so each estimate carries its own random error. Across many trials those errors cancel. Averaged over repeated trials, the estimator gives the true effect. That is what it means for the estimator to be unbiased. It says nothing about how close any one trial is.

Name it

Unbiased estimator. The difference in mean outcomes between treated and control units, under random assignment, satisfies:

E[ τ̂ ] = ATE, where τ̂ = mean(Y | treated) − mean(Y | control)
Var( τ̂ ) = σ1²/n1 + σ0²/n0  →  SD( τ̂ ) = √( 2(σ1² + σ0²) / n ) ∝ 1/√n

Quadrupling n halves the SD, so precision costs sample size at a diminishing rate. The SD predicted by theory now appears with the other readouts above: compare it with the SDs you wrote down. When treatment depends on baseline scores, as with the selection control, the errors no longer cancel, and the estimator is biased.

Exit question

Two dissimilar students are in a trial: Maya scores 70 without tutoring, Sam scores 35. Tutoring helps them differently: it adds +2 for Maya and +8 for Sam, so the average effect of tutoring on these two students is +5. A coin flip decides who is tutored, and the trial's estimate is the tutored student's score minus the other student's score.

What are the two possible estimates? Is this estimator biased for the average effect? What happens to the average estimate as the trial is repeated many times?

Agree on one answer as a group and write it once.

A model answer

The two estimates are +37 and −27. If Maya is tutored, the estimate is 72 − 35 = +37. If Sam is tutored, it is 43 − 70 = −27. Neither matches the average effect of +5, and neither matches either student's own effect (+2 or +8), because the two students differ so much at baseline.

The estimator is still unbiased for the average effect. The coin makes the two outcomes equally likely, so the expected estimate is (37 + (−27)) / 2 = +5, which is (2 + 8) / 2, the average of the two individual effects. Unbiasedness comes from random assignment, not from a large or well-matched sample, and it holds even at n = 2. Effects that differ between students do not break it; the target is their average.

Over many repeated trials, the average estimate settles on +5. The expected bias is zero at every number of trials. The gap you observe is chance, and it shrinks roughly like the trial-to-trial SD divided by the square root of the number of trials. Here both estimates sit 32 points either side of +5, so the SD is 32 and the gap is about 3.2 after 100 trials and about 0.32 after 10,000.

Tell the school: a two-student trial is unbiased but nearly useless on its own. Only n, the number of students per trial, shrinks the spread of each estimate.

Assumptions and what is left out
  • Parameter. 2000 students. Score without tutoring ~ Normal(50, 10). Individual effect ~ Normal(5, 4). True ATE about 5 points. Students per trial runs from 2 to 400 in even steps. Trials default to 200.
  • Distribution. Each trial draws n students with replacement and treats exactly n/2 by random shuffle. Var = v1/(n/2) + v0/(n/2), where v1 and v0 are the population variances of Y(1) and Y(0). At n = 2 the SD is about 14.7 points. The theory SD readout appears only once the reveal opens.
  • Simplification. No dropout, spillovers or noncompliance. Potential outcomes are all known, which is possible only in a simulation.
  • Simplification. The selection control ranks students on a noisy baseline score and treats the top half. At 0 it is a pure lottery. The reveal warns when selection is above 0.
  • Data. None: everything is simulated by R in the background.
  • Interpretation. Unbiased means the average over repeated trials equals the ATE. It is not a claim about one trial, and it does not depend on n.
  • Interpretation. Check my predictions opens after runs of at least 200 trials at two different n and a run with selection above 0. Feedback says what the simulation shows and quotes the observed SDs at n and 4n when both were run with selection 0.
  • Interpretation. The exit question uses a hand-built pair: Maya (baseline 70, effect +2) and Sam (baseline 35, effect +8), with a fair coin. The effects differ, and their average is +5. The two possible estimates are +37 and −27, which average to +5.
  • Interpretation. Expected bias over repeated trials is exactly zero. The observed gap between the mean of the estimates and the truth is noise of size about SD/√(trials). For the pair the SD is 32, so the gap is about 32/√(trials); in the simulated population the SD at n = 2 is about 14.7. That is the sense in which it approaches zero.
