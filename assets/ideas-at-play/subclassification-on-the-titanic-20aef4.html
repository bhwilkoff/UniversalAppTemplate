Subclassification on the Titanic

Haven't read it? Here's what you need

To compare first class with everyone else fairly, you subclassify: split people into strata that share sex and age, compare survival inside each stratum, then average those differences.

The weights choose the target. Each stratum's share of everyone gives the ATE, its share of first class gives the ATT, and its share of the others gives the ATU. An estimate needs both groups in every stratum it weights (common support).

Assumptions behind the numbers
  • Data. Dawson (1995), the book's titanic.dta: 2,201 people by class, sex, age group and survival. Controls pool second class, third class and crew (885 of the 1,876).
  • Simplification. Sex and a two-level age group are the only covariates. Unconfoundedness given them is assumed, not tested.
  • Parameter. Estimates weight the within-stratum differences by Nk/N (ATE), Nk1/N1 (ATT) or Nk0/N0 (ATU). No standard errors.
  • Interpretation. An estimate is n/a when a stratum it weights lacks either group: it can't be estimated from these data without extrapolating. One missing girl is a gap in the sample, not proof that such people can't exist.
  • Distribution. Last step: people fall into the vm strata at random, each first class with chance 325/2,201. The chance an estimate is computable is e to the minus the expected number of strata that block it.

Book notes: Table 5.2 prints 44 girls, where Eq. 5.18 and the counts give 45; its 0.138 for adult men comes from rounded rates (0.1374 exact); its ATT-weight sentence says "adult male stratum" where it means first class.

Left out: standard errors; regression, which fills empty cells by extrapolating; and continuous covariates such as exact age, where almost no cell holds both groups.
