Seven types of induction

Eight skeleton arguments, with the letters left as blanks. Each one is a form Wilkins gives for one of the seven types. Sort them by shape alone — there is no content to lean on.

Which type is each form?

Drag a card, or tap it and then tap a type. One type has two forms.

Induction is not the same as generalization

Look at where each form’s conclusion lands. A generalization runs from observed members to all members. A statistical syllogism runs the other way, from the whole group to one randomly selected member. An analogy stays at one level, from one thing to another. All three are inductive: the premises make the conclusion likely, never certain.

Wilkins says most inductive arguments fall into one or more of these types. A prediction is a kind of generalization; a sign works like an appeal to expertise. The forms are tidy; real arguments often are not.
