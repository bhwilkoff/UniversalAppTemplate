1. What would you predict, and from what?

A model can predict one column from other columns. The column you predict is the dependent variable; the columns you predict it from are the independent variables.

As a group: which column would you most like to predict, and which columns would you use? Name the dependent and the independent variables, and say why those independent variables might help.

Points to compare
  • The dependent variable is the one you want to know; the independent ones are what you already know when you predict.
  • Time order helps: 1974 and 1975 earnings are known before 1978, so they can predict re78. A model can't use information that arrives after the thing it predicts.
  • The study itself compared re78 between men with treat = 1 and treat = 0, to see whether training raised earnings.

Then: what do the two numbers mean?

As a group: what does the line predict for a man who earned $0 in 1974? Why might R call that number the “intercept”? If that's the intercept, where is the slope, and what does it mean in dollars?

Points to compare
  • At re74 = 0 the prediction is b0 ≈ $568: the height where the line crosses (intercepts) the vertical axis.
  • The slope is b1 ≈ 0.385: each extra $1,000 earned in 1974 adds about $385 to the predicted 1975 earnings. Move the dial by $1,000 and watch the prediction change by that much, wherever you start.
  • 326 of the 445 men earned $0 in 1974, and their average in 1975 was $304, not $568. One straight line has to serve every man, so it isn't exactly right even where the data are thickest.

5. Is it any good?

Someone says: “This is such a simple model. Is it accurate? Is it good? Is it useful?” These are reasonable questions.

As a group: how could you begin to answer them? Name at least one thing you would compute or look at, and what result would make you trust the model more.

Points to compare
  • Compare predictions with what happened. For each man, the miss (or error) is his actual 1975 earnings minus the line's prediction. Small misses mean an accurate model.
  • Compare with a simpler guess. Without re74, the best single guess for everyone is the average of re75. A model is useful only if it misses by less than that. On its own data a least-squares line always does at least as well as the average, so the question is by how much.
  • Try it on data it hasn't seen. A model can fit its own data well and still predict new men badly.
  • Ask “useful for what?” A miss of $2,000 may be fine for planning a program's budget and useless for deciding about one person.

7. Where else?

As a group: pick something from your own studies or life you'd like to predict from one other number (next term's grades from this term's, say, but find your own). What would the slope mean, in its units? How would you check that the line beats just guessing the average?

Points to compare
  • A good answer says the slope in units: “each extra point this term adds, say, 0.7 to next term's predicted grade”, as step 3 read 0.385 as $385 per extra $1,000.
  • The check is step 6's comparison: the line's typical miss (RMSE) against the miss from guessing the average for everyone. Better still, measure it on cases the line wasn't fitted to (step 5).
  • Then: is a miss that size small enough for what the prediction is for (step 5)? Fine for planning, perhaps; not for one person's decision.

You should now be able to:

  • name the dependent and independent variables and the parameters of a simple model (steps 1 and 4);
  • read a line's intercept as its prediction at zero, and its slope as the change in prediction per unit (step 3);
  • judge a model by its misses, compared with a simpler guess (steps 5 and 6).

Assumptions
  • datalalonde from the R package Matching: 445 men, earnings in dollars with cents as in the package.
  • simplificationOne independent variable and a straight line, fitted by least squares (lm). Standard errors, t values and p-values are left for later.
  • interpretationIn step 2, “about $10,000” means $9,000 to $11,000 in 1974.
  • parameterThe RMSE divides by n = 445. summary(model1) reports a slightly larger “residual standard error”, which divides by n − 2.
  • simplificationThe charts draw the same least-squares line from the same data in the page itself; a built-in check confirms it matches R's numbers.
