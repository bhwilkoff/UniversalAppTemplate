Angry or weary? Violence and attitudes to peace in Darfur

In 2003–04 the government of Sudan and the Janjaweed militia it backed attacked villages across Darfur, in western Sudan. Years later, researchers surveyed 1,276 Darfurian refugees in camps in eastern Chad. Some had been physically injured in the attacks; others had not. Did being harmed change how they felt about making peace?

Assumes multiple regression with control variables, and R² as the share of variation a variable explains. No sensitivity analysis needed beforehand.

How strong would a hidden confounder have to be?

First, from step 2. True or false: in the regression, the coefficient on directlyharmed is statistically significant at the 5% level.

True
False

The regression compares harmed and unharmed people from the same village. The researchers argue that within a village the violence was largely indiscriminate: bombings were too crude to pick out individuals, and attackers had little basis for choosing one villager over another. The one clear exception: women were targeted, often with sexual violence. That is why female is in the model.

Still, suppose something unmeasured, call it Z, made some people both more likely to be harmed and more pro-peace. Then part of the 0.097 would be Z, not harm. We can’t measure Z, but we can ask how strong it would need to be to change our conclusion, in either of two ways: to bring the estimate all the way down to zero, or just to make it no longer statistically significant. Two numbers describe Z’s strength:

  • Strength with the treatment: the share of the leftover variation in being harmed (after the controls) that Z explains.
  • Strength with the outcome: the share of the leftover variation in peacefactor (after harm and the controls) that Z explains.

Both are partial R² values, between 0% and 100%.

Your prediction. Say Z explains the same share of both. What is the smallest share that would bring the estimate all the way down to zero?

Where along the diagonal does the estimate reach zero? Where does it stop being significant? The two strengths start locked together; drag either slider. Then unlock them: can a confounder that is very strong on one side but weak on the other do the same damage?

From the dials: what equal strength brings the estimate to zero? Answer to the nearest whole percent.

The bias a confounder causes depends only on these two strengths and on numbers the regression already reports:

bias = SE × √( df × R²Y~Z × R²D~Z / (1 − R²D~Z) )

Here SE is the standard error (0.0233), df the residual degrees of freedom (783), and R²Y~Z, R²D~Z Z’s partial R² with the outcome and with the treatment. Every point on the chart is this formula.

Is a confounder that strong plausible? Compare it with gender

A robustness value of 13.9% means nothing until we know whether a confounder that strong is likely. Observed covariates give a yardstick. Gender was the main known basis for who was targeted, and it strongly predicts attitudes to peace. In this data, after the other controls:

Even three times gender leaves the estimate positive, at about a third of its size; only there does it lose significance (t = 1.63). The analysis does not prove no such confounder exists. It turns an open-ended worry (“there might be confounding”) into one specific question: is there an unmeasured factor three times as strongly tied to being harmed, and to attitudes, as gender? Given how the attacks were carried out, the authors argue that is implausible.

2. Run the sensitivity analysis

Run the call and read the printout. Then store the two robustness values from step 3, as proportions (0.05 means 5%). Both are in the table darfur_sens$sensitivity_stats:

  • rv: the robustness value, the equal strength (with treatment and outcome) a confounder needs to bring the estimate to zero. It is the column rv_q (q = 1: explain away 100% of the estimate).
  • rv_sig: the robustness value for significance, the equal strength a confounder needs to make the estimate no longer statistically significant at 5%. It is the column rv_qa (a for alpha = 0.05).

How should the finding be reported?

As a group: which sentence belongs in the paper?

Being harmed causes more pro-peace attitudes; with p < 0.001 this is established.
Harmed respondents are more pro-peace (+0.097). A confounder as strong as gender, or even three times as strong, could not reverse this, though at three times it could make it no longer significant.
The estimate is fragile: any unobserved confounder could explain it away.
The sensitivity analysis shows there is no unobserved confounding.

The second sentence is the honest one. The first ignores confounding: a p-value says nothing about bias. The third throws away what the analysis found: “any” confounder cannot explain it, only one stronger than 13.9% on both sides. The fourth claims too much: sensitivity analysis never shows that confounding is absent. It says how strong confounding would have to be, and leaves the reader to judge whether that is plausible, with gender as the yardstick.

Exit question: in your own words, what does a robustness value of 13.9% tell a skeptical reader? Two sentences.

Where else would you ask how strong a confounder must be?

As a group: pick a finding from your own studies or life that compares people who did and didn’t get something (students who used a tutoring centre, say, but find your own). Which hidden factor worries you most, and which measured factor would you use as its yardstick, the way gender was used here? Could the hidden one plausibly be several times stronger?

Points to compare
  • A good answer names a factor tied to both who got the treatment and the outcome. One tied to only one side causes no bias: the bias depends on the product of the two strengths (step 3).
  • The yardstick is a measured variable whose two strengths you can compute, as gender’s 0.9% and 12.5% were (step 4). A good yardstick is a measured factor that is linked to both the treatment and the outcome, as gender was linked to who was targeted and to attitudes to peace.
  • The check is a comparison, not a yes or no: is the hidden factor plausibly as strong as that yardstick, or several times stronger? That is what the report on step 6 states.

You should now be able to:

  • read a robustness value as the equal strength a confounder needs to bring an estimate to zero, or below significance (step 3);
  • benchmark a hypothetical confounder against an observed covariate, on the chart and with sensemakr (steps 4 and 5);
  • report a finding with how strong confounding would have to be to change it (step 6).
