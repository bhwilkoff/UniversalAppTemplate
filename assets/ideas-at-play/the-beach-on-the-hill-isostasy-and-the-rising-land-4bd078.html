The beach on the hill

On Sweden's High Coast, wave-rounded beach stones lie in the forest. The highest old shoreline is about 285 m above today's sea. The sea never stood that high. So the land rose. How does solid rock rise?

1. Does a thick slab of crust press down, or float?

Mountain belts are made of the same kind of rock as the plains beside them, only thicker. Under Tibet the crust is about 70 km thick; under most continents, about 35 km.

Its top stands higher, and its base reaches deeper.
Its top stands higher; its base rests at the same depth as the plains' crust.
Its extra weight presses it down, so its top stands no higher.

2. How deep is the root of a 5 km mountain?

The crust floats on the mantle, which is denser (3.3 g/cm³ against 2.8). Deep down, every column must weigh the same. So each km of crust standing above the plains is balanced by a root of light crust pushed down into the heavy mantle.

In Python: a km of root replaces mantle with crust, which saves rho_mantle - rho_crust of weight. Find the root that balances height km of crust on top. Run it and your root appears in the cross-section.

Hint 1

Set root * (rho_mantle - rho_crust) equal to height * rho_crust and solve for root.

Hint 2

Divide height * rho_crust by the density difference.

Isostasy. Crust floats on the mantle, like ice on water. A mountain is held up by its root, about 5.6 times as deep as the mountain is high.

3. The ice melts. What does the land do next?

About 20,000 years ago an ice sheet as big as today's Greenland ice sheet, which is up to 3 km thick, covered Scandinavia. Its weight pushed the land down into the mantle. The ice left the High Coast about 9,600 years ago.

Predict. Once the ice is gone, what does the land under it do?

It stays where it is: the mantle is solid rock.
It springs back to its old height at once.
It keeps rising for thousands of years.
It sinks further.

4. Which mantle leaves a beach on a hill?

The mantle carries earthquake waves that only a solid can carry. Yet the land rose. Try the mantle three ways.

The mantle

As a group: which mantle could have lifted a beach 285 m above the sea?

One that flows like water
Solid rock that flows very slowly
Solid rock that never flows

Solid, but it flows. The land was still low when the sea first reached it, so the first beaches formed low down; the slow mantle then lifted them for thousands of years. A mantle that never flows can't let the ice push the land down at all, and one that flows like water is back up before the first beach forms. To an earthquake wave the mantle is solid; over thousands of years it flows.

With the ice melting over about 8,000 years (step 3), the first beach comes out near the real 285 m and the land rises about 7 mm a year today; the real figure is about 8. Much of the rise happened while the ice was still thinning. The model holds sea level fixed.

Assumptions
  • Parameter: densities 2.8 g/cm³ (crust), 3.3 g/cm³ (mantle), 0.9 g/cm³ (ice).
  • Simplification: Airy isostasy. Each column floats on its own; the plate's stiffness is ignored.
  • Simplification: the land moves toward balance with the ice on it at a rate set by a 4,000-year time constant. "Flows like water" balances at once; "never flows" never moves.
  • Simplification: the ice thins at a steady rate over the melting time you set, sea level stays fixed, and the land sinks evenly under the ice. Heights in the coast picture are exaggerated about 50 times.
  • Data: High Coast highest shoreline about 285 m, ice gone about 9,600 years ago, uplift about 8 mm a year (UNESCO World Heritage listing 898); about 1 cm a year near Hudson Bay (GPS: Sella et al. 2007, Geophysical Research Letters); about 70 km of crust under southern Tibet; the Scandinavian ice sheet comparable in volume to today's Greenland ice sheet (Lane et al. 2020, citing Patton et al. 2016).

Left out: the plate's flexure, the bulge that sinks around a melting ice sheet, and the sea rising as ice melts.
