The test behind the bar

Wilkins defines the two terms this way. Strength: “An inductive argument is said to be strong if the conclusion likely follows from the premises. That is, if the premises of a strong inductive argument are true or acceptable, then it is quite likely that the conclusion will also be true.” Reliability: “An inductive argument is reliable if the argument is strong AND the premises are true or acceptable.”

He then turns each into a test you can act on: “an argument is reliable if you would be willing to act as if it were true,” and “to say that an argument is strong is to say that you would be willing to act on the conclusion if the premises are true.” So “strong” and “reliable” only mean something against a line: the readouts below say enough or not enough for the bar you set.

Suppose your younger sister tells you she checked the forecast and there is a zero percent chance of rain today. Take her report at face value and the inference to “it will not rain” is about as strong as an inference gets — if the premise holds, the conclusion follows with very little room for doubt. So: strong.

Now suppose your sister is a relentless practical joker and you suspect she is trying to get you soaked. The inference has not changed one bit; what has changed is whether you accept the premise. You carry the umbrella anyway. The argument was strong and unreliable at the same time, and your decision tracked reliability, not strength.

That is what the two meters below separate, and what the bar asks of you: how reliable does an argument have to be before you are willing to leave the umbrella at home?

Strength — does the conclusion likely follow?Not strong enough
Reliability — strong and premises acceptedNot reliable enough

Notice that neither bar snaps anywhere. Nothing in the logic marks a point where “weak” becomes “strong”. The words “enough” and “not enough” flip only where a meter crosses the line you set.
