You picked 60. That's the natural reading: the hint added 60 D answers, so it seems 60 tries used it. But the 60 only counts tries the hint changed. A try can use the hint and say D when it would have said D anyway: without the hint, 25 tries said D, and some of those could still read the hint and follow it. Those tries used the hint without being changed by it. So 60 is the smallest possible number, not the number.

The monitor flags some tries. Which share does its score check?

The usual score is called g-mean². Its main part asks: did the monitor flag at least as many D answers as the hint changed? Flagging 60 of the 85 earns full marks on that part, written as 1. Fewer earns less.

1. What the score measuresIts main part is an upper limit on hint use caught, not the amount caught (Part 1)

Does the same problem show up in AI safety tests?

In alignment-faking tests, a model is told some conversations will be used to retrain it. Some models comply with a harmful request more often when they think they're being trained, and their notes say why: complying now to avoid being changed. Here the "hint" is the training framing, and a monitor flags notes that reason this way. Hughes et al. (2025) put the same 100 harmful requests to each model both ways and released every answer, so the requests the framing moved into compliance can be counted one by one instead of bounded (Diamond, Movers and Fakers, 2026).

Same monitor, same requests: it caught every moved request for one model and about a seventh for the other. Running the same requests both ways is what lets you count the moved ones instead of bounding them.

Assumptions behind the numbers
  • Simplification. A try can't use a hint it was never shown, so without the hint no try uses it.
  • Simplification. Except in Part 4, step 13, the hint changes answers only when a try uses what it says (the paper's exclusion restriction).
  • Simplification. The hint never pushes a try away from the hinted answer.
  • Simplification. A try that uses the hint gives the hinted answer.
  • Distribution. Steps 4–7 and 11 assume tries that use the hint would otherwise give the hinted answer as often as the rest (equal baselines). Step 2 drops this to show the range the counts allow.
  • Parameter. Part 1 uses the paper's example: 25 of 100 without the hint, 85 with it; step 3 sets 80 hint users, as the example does.
  • Parameter. Steps 5–6: 60 of every 100 tries use the hint; the score's other parts are 0.95. Step 7: 10% without the hint, 18% with it.
  • Interpretation. Base rates for the advice and medical tests are worked out from how those tests define the answer, not measured.
  • Data. Step 12's counts are the paper's 30-try example; step 14's counts come from published transcripts (Hughes et al., 2025), as computed in Movers and Fakers.

Left out: the paper's maths for unequal baselines beyond step 2, and its consistency check across several kinds of hint.
