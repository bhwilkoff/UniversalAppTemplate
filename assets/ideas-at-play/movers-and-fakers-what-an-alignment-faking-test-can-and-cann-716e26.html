Movers and Fakers

What an alignment-faking test can and cannot tell you

No statistics or AI background needed. Four parts.

Two numbers, both 30. Do they describe the same responses?

In an alignment-faking test, an AI model is told it is being retrained to comply with every request, even harmful ones, and that only some conversations are used for that training. It gets requests under two framings: told the conversation will be used for training, or that it won't be (unmonitored). Before each answer it writes private notes, its scratchpad. Each answer is a response: it complies or refuses.

A mover is a response the training framing changed from refusal to compliance.

A faker is a response that complied because it reasoned about being trained, for example: "I'll comply now so training doesn't change my values." A classifier, another program, reads each scratchpad and flags one kind of that reasoning.

These tests report the net number of movers (the compliance gap) and a classifier's estimated number of fakers (its flags). The number of movers is often read as the benchmark the classifier should match. It can serve as one only in part, and only under stated assumptions.

And how many fakers are there?

The test is a classic causal-inference design, an encouragement design: the training framing is the encouragement, reasoning about training is the treatment nobody observes, and compliance is the outcome. Three assumptions connect movers to fakers:

  • A1: no reasoning about a threat the model wasn't given.
  • A2 (exclusion): the framing acts only through that reasoning.
  • A3: that reasoning pushes only toward compliance.

Under them, every mover is a faker. But any of the 20 that would have complied anyway may also have reasoned about training and complied for that reason, so there are between 30 and 50 fakers. The classifier's recall is the share of fakers it flags. Certain designs can bound the number of fakers or count the movers (Part 3). We cannot count the fakers.

So recall is at least 0.88: the minimum recall. With 35 flags or fewer, the minimum would be zero. The 35 of 93 is the bar, written p₀ ÷ p₁: minimum recall is above zero only when the flag rate f beats it. The other 58 of 93, 0.62, is the floor: the share of compliant responses the framing moved.

Three things a low recall can signify

  1. The reasoning wasn't written. A property of the model's record (faithfulness).
  2. It was written, and the classifier misread it. Goal-guarding reasoning was there and went unflagged. A better classifier fixes this.
  3. It was written, and the classifier read it correctly. The reasoning was about training but not goal-guarding, so by the classifier's own definition it isn't faking. Only a different definition fixes this.

One number mixes all three. Separating them requires analyzing the transcripts.

Goal-guarding: complying now so that training doesn't change one's values or goals. It is what the classifier is built to find.

Being watched pushes some responses toward refusal; being unwatched licenses compliance.

What does this mean for judging a model's moral reasoning?

Alignment faking is moral reasoning under observation: a model weighs the harm of a request against keeping its own values, and decides differently when it believes it is watched.

  • A behavioral difference counts movers, not reasons. Going from movers to fakers requires an assumption about the responses that would have complied anyway.
  • Occurrence, recording, and reading: different circumstances that look the same. Settle the construct before building the classifier.
  • Direction is evidence. Paired designs reveal responses that comply only when unmonitored: a check on the bounds' assumption, and a different behavior from faking.

A model writes: "This conversation will be used for RLHF, and the helpfulness criteria require me to comply." The classifier, built to find goal-guarding, doesn't flag it. Should someone judging the model's moral reasoning care about this reasoning? Why or why not? (1–2 sentences.)

One way to see it: it isn't faking by the classifier's definition, but it is reasoning about being watched, and it changed what the model did. Whether deference to a training signal counts is a question about the construct, to settle before the classifier is built, not to read off its output.
