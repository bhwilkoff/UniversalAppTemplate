Rawls and AI

What political liberalism asks of AI governance

From Margaret Jenkins's talk at SEPAI 2026 (Milan) and her paper “Applying Rawls’s Justice Theory to AI Alignment.” No philosophy background needed.

Who decides what an AI system should value?

John Rawls (1921–2002) rarely wrote about technology. But AI raises questions at the heart of political philosophy, about legitimacy, pluralism, coercion, sovereignty and freedom, and his ideas already shape influential work on justice and AI (Gabriel 2022; Weidinger et al. 2023).

Justice as fairness is Rawls's theory. The principles of justice are the ones a fair procedure would select, and a procedure is fair when its terms are acceptable to persons who are free and equal. Think of the rules of a game: fair rules are acceptable to all players, not set by the most powerful.

“These are terms each participant may reasonably accept, and sometimes should accept, provided that everyone else likewise accepts them.”Rawls 2001, 6

Reasonable pluralism: when people use their reason freely, they reach different religious, philosophical and moral views, and reasonable people go on disagreeing. So no single doctrine can form the basis for public life.

Political liberalism is Rawls's later work, which starts from that fact. Its aim is both moral and practical: principles that people can agree to willingly despite deep disagreement.

Basic rights and liberties have lexical priority: they must be secured before the other aims are weighed at all, the way alphabetical order settles the first letter before it looks at the second. Both of Rawls's theories, for a single society and between societies, give them this priority.

So AI governance should first protect them, and urgently close the blind spots and gaps in legal and regulatory frameworks, so that AI protects rather than violates these rights. The other three are not dismissed:

“All of these may matter, and may even be critically important but they would not be prioritized from the perspective of justice as fairness, especially given substantiated concerns that AI pose certain threats to basic rights and liberties.”Jenkins, “Applying Rawls’s Justice Theory to AI Alignment”

Why do basic rights come before everything else?

The basic structure is a society's main institutions taken together: its laws, government, courts and economy. It applies to everyone, with little option to leave, and provides the background against which individuals, civil society and private enterprise operate.

Rawls calls it “the social world in which our character and our conception of ourselves as persons … are first acquired” (1993, 41). Its principles “must give priority to those basic freedoms and opportunities … that enable us to become free and equal citizens in the first place” (1993, 41).

Jenkins's conclusion: AI, as part of this social world, would need to be harnessed toward that end and give it lexical priority over any other objectives, including other objectives of justice.

And distributive justice?

Besides lacking priority, Rawls's principle for distribution (the difference principle, step 7) is not focused on outcome distribution: it is structural, not allocative (it concerns how institutions are set up, not who gets which share). Jenkins notes that Gabriel's theory of justice for AI downplays or overlooks this.

What does Rawls's equality rest on?

The original position is Rawls's thought experiment for choosing principles of justice. The parties choose behind a veil of ignorance: they don't know their place in society (rich or poor, their religion, their talents), so nobody can tilt the rules in their own favour.

The parties represent persons as free and equal, each with two moral powers: a capacity for a sense of justice, and a capacity to form, revise and pursue a view of what is good in life.

That symmetry is not a given. Rawls defends it as a device of representation, built on clearly specified assumptions about who the parties are and why they count equally. And in his theory for a single society, only individual persons are represented, though many other stakeholders are affected by the principles chosen.

Who the assumptions leave out

Critics point out that Rawls's thought experiment excludes some people: those with cognitive impairments, and others who may not have the capacity to be rational or reasonable (Nussbaum 2006; Kittay 1999). Jenkins counts these among the important critiques that any use of public reason to work out AI governance needs to wrestle with.

One theory for every subject?

“Each kind of subject — whether an institution or an individual, whether a political society or a society of political societies — may be governed by its own characteristic principles.”Rawls 1993, 63

Thomas Nagel calls this Rawls's anti-monism: his two principles of justice are designed to regulate “neither the personal conduct of individuals living in a just society, nor the governance of private associations, nor the international relations of societies to one another” (Nagel 2005, 123).

Rawls's method is constructivist: he builds each theory step by step from its own starting values and devices of representation, rooted in values widely shared in a specific public culture, so that the people to be governed by the principles can make sense of them and endorse them.

The difference principle

Rawls's difference principle allows social and economic inequalities only if they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. Gabriel argues that while it “is most clearly applicable to AI systems that have been integrated into core economic functions,” it “potentially has much wider applicability, extending to the full range of sociotechnical systems that shape a person’s access to resources or impact upon their social standing and sense of self-worth” (2022, 226). Jenkins replies that Rawls would not necessarily defend this move (he explicitly rejected the difference principle for the international case), and that it ignores his constructivist method.

A lesson from history

In the 1970s and 80s, Charles Beitz and Thomas Pogge applied Rawls's theory to international justice. They were surprised when Rawls's own The Law of Peoples (1999), his theory of justice between societies, looked very different: its central units are peoples, not individual persons, with a new constructivist method and different principles.

Anti-monism is familiar from ordinary life. A Catholic nun may support norms for her parish that she would not endorse for public life.

Does public reason silence people?

Public reason: when citizens justify the principles that will govern everyone, they offer reasons that others with different views can reasonably accept, not reasons that depend on any one comprehensive doctrine (a whole religious, philosophical or moral view of life).

Gabriel and Keeling worry that Rawls's requirements for public reason are “onerous” and “could prevent many of those affected by AI from surfacing claims that are couched in simpler notions of well-being, autonomy or rights” (2025, 1960).

On Jenkins's reading, people can surface their own values and claims, live by them, use their own terms and voices, and defend public policy to themselves and others on that basis. What public reason asks is narrower: ideally, as part of citizenship, people look for reasons to justify principles for governing AI alignment (and other institutions) that satisfy reciprocity in a pluralist society. Political liberalism aims to start from inclusive, non-intimidating ideas that can be shared and easily understood.

“A zeal for the whole truth tempts us to a broader and deeper unity that cannot be justified by public reason.”Rawls 1993, 43

Michael Sandel (1994) criticises public reason from the other direction: it is too simple and reductive, stripping away the richer, controversial and deep values people have and should bring to politics. Jenkins counts this among the critiques any use of public reason for AI governance needs to wrestle with.

Beyond one society, and beyond the rules

Lesson 4: Preventing catastrophic harm

Two assumptions motivated Rawls's Law of Peoples: the worst suffering follows from “political injustice with its own cruelties and callousness,” and the gravest injustices can be eliminated by “following just social policies” and establishing “just basic institutions” (Rawls 1999, 7). Jenkins sees multilateral efforts such as the Joint Declaration on the Paris Call for AI Safety and Security (November 2026) as aligned in spirit, but they need a concrete roadmap, institutional enforcement and buy-in from the major AI powers.

A closing thought

Whatever principles we adopt, AI governance will not only regulate AI. Rawls held that institutions and norms socialize us and constitute who we are, through “moral learning”:

“We have no prior identity before being in society.”Rawls 1996, 41

The choices we make about AI and its governance will shape who we become. It is up to us to reflect on who we wish to be.

Take an AI rule you have met: a chatbot that refuses some requests, a school's rule on AI in homework, an app that ranks posts. What might living under it teach people to expect of each other? (1–2 sentences.)
