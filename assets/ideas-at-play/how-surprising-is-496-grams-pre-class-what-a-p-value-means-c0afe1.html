How Surprising Is 496 Grams?

A machine should fill cereal boxes with 500 g on average. A sample of 25 boxes averages 496 g. Is that evidence the machine short-fills, or just chance? One short R simulation helps you decide.

Assumes: the sampling distribution of the sample mean, the standard error SE = σ ÷ √n, and pressing Run on R code. There is one short R edit.

The facts

  • The machine is set to fill boxes with 500 g on average.
  • Single boxes vary: from years of records, their SD is σ = 10 g.
  • A consumer group weighs a random sample of n = 25 boxes. Their average: x̄ = 496 g.

Two explanations

  • Null hypothesis, H₀: μ = 500 g. The machine is fine; this sample came out low by chance.
  • Alternative hypothesis, HA: μ < 500 g. The machine short-fills.

μ is the machine's true average fill, which nobody knows. HA says "less than" because the group suspected short-filling before weighing anything.

1. Is the machine short-filling?

A hypothesis test starts by assuming H₀ is true and asks: would a sample like ours be surprising then?

3. What does that share tell the consumer group?

The share you counted in step 2, about 0.02, estimates the p-value: the probability, if H₀ is true, of getting a sample mean at least as extreme as ours (here: 496 g or less, the direction HA points).

In one or two sentences of your own: what does p ≈ 0.02 say about the group's sample of 25 boxes?

One way to say it. If the machine really averaged 500 g (H₀ true), only about 2% of random samples of 25 boxes would average 496 g or less. A sample this low would be surprising under H₀, so it is evidence against H₀.

Compare with yours: does it start from "if H₀ were true"? A p-value is worked out assuming H₀, so it cannot be the chance that H₀ is true. That mix-up is the most common one.

One tail or two? HA: μ < 500 points one way, so only low averages count as "at least as extreme": one tail, p ≈ 0.02. If the question were "is the machine off in either direction?" (HA: μ ≠ 500), averages of 504 g or more would count too: two tails, p ≈ 0.04, about double. Choose the side from the question, before seeing the data.

4. When is a sample surprising enough?

The cut-off for "surprising" is set before the data are seen: the significance level α (alpha), usually 0.05. Then:

  • p < α: reject H₀. The result is statistically significant: evidence against H₀, in the direction HA points.
  • p ≥ α: fail to reject H₀. Not enough evidence for HA. This does not prove H₀.

For the group's sample, p ≈ 0.02 < 0.05: reject H₀. The data are evidence that the machine short-fills.
