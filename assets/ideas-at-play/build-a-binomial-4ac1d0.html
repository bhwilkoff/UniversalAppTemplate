Build a Binomial

Stack yes/no trials into a distribution, and read its centre, spread and shape.

Admitted students

Each admitted student accepts the offer with probability 0.7, independently of the others (Minerva's yield rate in the lesson). You meet 16 admitted students.

Each student is a Bernoulli trial: accept (1) or not (0). The number who accept, out of 16, is a binomial random variable.

2. The distribution of the count

Each bar is P(X = x): the chance that exactly x of the n trials are yeses. Bars up to k are filled; the dashed line is the mean and the shaded band one SD either side.

With n = 16 and p = 0.7: what is the chance that at least 12 of the 16 accept? Answer as a decimal, to two places.

3. When is a binomial lopsided?

As a group: which statement is right?

It's lopsided when p is far from 0.5, and the lopsidedness fades as n grows.
It's lopsided when n is small, whatever p is.
It's always symmetric around np.
It's lopsided whenever p ≠ 0.5, and more trials make it worse.

4. Where else?

As a group: pick a count from your own studies or life that adds up yes/no outcomes (free throws made in a game, say, but find your own). With rough guesses for n and p, what range of counts would you expect? If a real count fell outside it, which would you check first: that every trial has the same chance p, or that the trials are independent?

Points to compare
  • Typical is np; a count more than about two SDs, 2√(np(1 − p)), away is a surprise (step 2). With n = 20 and p = 0.6 that is 12, and anything outside about 8 to 16.
  • A good check names how the condition would fail for that count: streaks (one miss rattles the next shot) break independence; a chance that drifts (tiredness, harder questions late on) breaks the same p.

You should now be able to:

  • find a binomial's mean np, its SD √(np(1 − p)) and the chance of “at least k” (step 2);
  • say when a binomial is lopsided and how more trials change that (step 3).

Assumptions
  • distributionX ~ Binomial(n, p): n independent trials, each a yes with the same probability p. P(X = x) = C(n, x) pˣ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ˣ.
  • parameterDefaults n = 16, p = 0.7 (the lesson's admitted students). n from 1 to 60, p from 0.05 to 0.95.
  • simplification“Run the n trials once” uses a seeded generator (mulberry32); the seed is shared so a group sees the same run.
  • interpretation“Most likely” is the mode, ⌊(n + 1)p⌋. When (n + 1)p is a whole number there are two modes, (n + 1)p − 1 and (n + 1)p, and the lower is shown.
