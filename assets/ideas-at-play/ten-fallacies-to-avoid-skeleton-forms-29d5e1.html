Ten fallacies to avoid

Ten skeleton arguments, with the letters left as blanks. Each one has the shape of a common fallacy: a flaw in how the premises support the conclusion. Sort them by shape alone — there is no content to lean on.

A fallacy is a flaw in the support, not proof that the conclusion is false

In the words of the list these definitions come from: “A fallacious argument may nevertheless have a true conclusion; the defect lies in the inadequate reasoning offered in support of it.” Concluding that a claim is false because someone argued for it badly is itself on the list, as the argument from fallacy.

Several of these are damaged versions of good inductive forms. A hasty generalization is a generalization from too little; deferring to a title is the bad twin of a legitimate argument from expertise; post hoc mistakes a sequence in time for a causal inference. The shape alone rarely settles it: what matters is the evidence behind each premise.
