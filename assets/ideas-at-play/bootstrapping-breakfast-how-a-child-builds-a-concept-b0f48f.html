    Did the child end up with the family’s concept?

    The family used “breakfast” in two ways. For the sibling it was the first meal after sleep, whatever the clock said. For the parent it was a kind of food, which can be eaten at dinner. One meaning cannot do both jobs: try it below.

    So the last event was not more of the same. Up to event 6, each event added or removed features; the sibling’s 1 PM breakfast removed two at once. Event 7 changed the structure: one meaning became two related ones, and “breakfast for dinner” only makes sense to someone who has both.

    Name it. The worksheet’s title echoes Susan Carey’s account of bootstrapping (The Origin of Concepts, 2009): a learner first holds a word as an almost empty placeholder, then fills in its meaning from how it is used around them. A word with several related meanings is polysemous.
