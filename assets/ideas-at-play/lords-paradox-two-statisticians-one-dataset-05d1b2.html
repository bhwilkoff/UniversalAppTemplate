Lord's Paradox

Two statisticians analyze the same data and reach different conclusions. Run the code, read each claim and decide whether you agree.

What is the setting?

From Holland and Rubin, On Lord's Paradox, 1983:

A large university is interested in investigating the effects on the students of the diet provided in the university dining halls and any sex differences in these effects. Various types of data are gathered. In particular, the weight of each student at the time of his [or her] arrival in September and his [or her] weight the following June are recorded.

The key elements of Lord's paradox

  • The data: each student's weight in September and again in June, and each student's sex.
  • The treatment: the dining-hall diet. Every student eats it, so no one is observed on any other diet.
  • The question: did the diet affect weight, and did it affect men and women differently?
  • Two standard analyses: one compares each group's change in weight from September to June; the other compares men's and women's June weights after adjusting for September weight.
  • The paradox: applied to the same data, both analyses are computed correctly, yet they reach different conclusions.
  • Your task: work out why, and what it would take to decide which conclusion, if either, to believe.

Make sure everyone in your group understands the setting. Raise your hand if you have questions. We will call the two analysts Statistician 1 and Statistician 2.

What does this page assume and leave out?
  • Data: six invented students, three women and three men, from the Holland and Rubin setting.
  • Models: Statistician 1 fits june - sept = a + b·female. Statistician 2 fits june = a + c·sept + b·female.
  • Simplification: no noise, so every line fits exactly; no standard errors are read.
  • Interpretation: the diet is the treatment; sex is an attribute, not a manipulable treatment.
  • Style: plots use palette colors with different shapes in place of red and blue, so color is never the only cue.

Left out: sampling variability and formal methods for non-randomized data.

Lord's paradox

Both statisticians computed correctly. Fitted to this data, Statistician 1's coefficient on female would be 0 and Statistician 2's would be -15. They disagree because each fills in the missing Y(0), the June weight without the dining-hall diet, with a different assumption.

Statistician 1 (change score) assumes each student would have stayed at their September weight. Statistician 2 (adjusting for September weight) assumes that without the diet, men and women with the same September weight would have had the same June weight, following one line of slope 0.5, so the 15 lb gap between the observed lines is credited to the diet. The data are identical and cannot tell which assumption is correct.

change score: june - sept = a + b*female   adjusted: june = a + c*sept + b*female

This is Lord's paradox: both analyses target the same causal question, the difference between the diet's effect on women and on men, and the answer depends on an assumption about the counterfactual that the numbers cannot check.
