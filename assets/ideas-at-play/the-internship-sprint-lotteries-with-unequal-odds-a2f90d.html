The Internship Sprint

Random assignment with unequal probabilities, and students who don't take part.

MEMO · Office of the Dean · Re: Internship Sprint pilot

The career center wanted to try a new program, the six-week Internship Sprint (résumé rebuild, three mock interviews with alumni, introductions to employers). They picked 1,100 sophomores at random from our 6,000, then split those students at random into a treatment group, offered a seat, and a control group, not offered one. Only students in the treatment group could take part, and not all of them did.

2. What happens inside each major?

Within a major everyone had the same probability of assignment, so there random assignment is a fair test. Assignment to treatment raised the internship rate by 12 points for humanities majors (41% vs 29%) and by 4 points for STEM majors (62% vs 58%).

For one number for the whole sample, weight each major by how many students it has: (600 × 12 + 500 × 4) ÷ 1,100 = 8.4 points. This is the average effect of assignment to treatment on getting an internship: the intention-to-treat effect (ITT), also called the ATE of assignment.

The memo's pooled number doesn't distinguish between the majors: it averages the same rates with different weights, because the treatment group is humanities majors and the control group .

4. The effect on those who took part (ATT)

Joining raised the rate by 24 points for humanities majors and 5 for STEM majors. 400 students joined: 240 humanities and 160 STEM majors.

What was the average effect of the Sprint on the 400 who joined, in points? This is the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT).

Two random draws, in this order

From the sample to all 6,000. Random sampling makes the sample's mix (54.5% humanities) and each major's effects estimates for all sophomores. So if every sophomore were assigned to treatment, the best estimate of the ATT is about 13 points, the same weighting as in step 4.

That needs one more assumption that no randomization supplies: students would join at the same rates (50% and 80%) when everyone is assigned to treatment. Joiners in a small pilot with personal invitations may not be the joiners of a campus-wide program.

Assumptions behind this page
  • dataThe case and every number in it are invented for teaching.
  • simplificationEach major's internship rates in the treatment group (41%, 62%) and the control group (29%, 58%) stay the same when the sliders change the probabilities of assignment; the sliders change only how many students fall in each row.
  • simplificationNobody could join without being assigned to treatment (one-sided non-compliance), so within a major the effect on joiners is also the effect on everyone who took part.
  • interpretationAssignment to treatment affects internships only through joining: no-shows gain nothing from having been assigned.
  • parameterTake-up in the treatment group: 50% for humanities and arts, 80% for STEM and business, at any probability of assignment.
  • interpretationFor all 6,000 sophomores: students would join at the same rates if everyone were assigned to treatment.
  • simplificationSampling error is ignored: every number is shown as if it were exact.

Left out: standard errors and confidence intervals, and two-sided non-compliance (control-group students who find a way in).
