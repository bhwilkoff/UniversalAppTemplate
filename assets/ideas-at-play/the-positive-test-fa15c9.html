From a news story

“The test picks up 95% of people who have COVID. So if you test positive, there's a 95% chance you're infected.”

3. What's wrong with the story?

As a group: what is the mistake?

It swaps “95% of infected people test positive” for “95% of people who test positive are infected”. The second depends on how common COVID is.
It forgets the false positives: the right answer is 95% − 10% = 85%.
Nothing: if the test catches 95% of cases, a positive result is 95% reliable.
It should use the false-negative rate, 5%, so the chance is 5%.

Three numbers behind a positive

  • How often it says yes when the thing is there: 95% for the COVID test.
  • How often it says yes when the thing isn't there: 10%.
  • How common the thing is among those tested: 2% this week.

Together: 19 true positives and 98 false ones in 1,000 people.

4. Your own test

As a group: pick a test or flag from your own studies or life that says yes or no (a plagiarism checker flagging an essay, say, but find your own). Before trusting a “yes” from it, which numbers would you look up, and how would your trust change if what it looks for were rare?

Points to compare
  • One number is not enough. A good answer names all three: how often it says yes when the thing is there, how often when it isn't, and how common the thing is among those tested (step 1).
  • Count out 1,000 cases, as in step 2: true yeses against false yeses. When the thing is rare, the false yeses from the much larger “no” group can outnumber the true ones, as in the rare-disease case.
  • “Catches 95% of cases” and “95% of its yeses are right” are different claims; only the second answers whether to trust a yes (step 3).

You should now be able to:

  • turn a test's error rates and how common a condition is into counts out of 1,000, and find the share of positives who have it (step 2);
  • say how that share moves as the condition gets rarer or more common, and roughly where a positive becomes more likely right than wrong (step 2);
  • spot a claim that swaps “share of the sick who test positive” for “share of positives who are sick” (step 3).

Assumptions
  • parameterFalse-positive rate 10% and false-negative rate 5%, from the pre-class poll. They don't change with the share who have COVID.
  • parameterShare of test-takers with COVID: 2% to 50% in steps of 2%, default 2%. Every count is then a whole number of people.
  • simplificationCounts are expected counts out of 1,000, rounded to whole people for the picture; the percentages use the unrounded counts.
  • interpretation“The chance you have COVID” is the share with COVID among the 1,000 who test positive, as if you were one of them picked at random.
