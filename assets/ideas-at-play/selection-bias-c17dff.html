Selection Bias Lives in Y(0)

Ten units, one targeting rule, and a naive comparison that is sometimes exactly right.

Work through it

  1. Treatment is targeted to the five units with the highest X, and X raises both the baseline and the effect. Predict before touching anything.
    Will the naive gap equal ATT?
  2. Now set a = 0 and keep b = 2. Treatment is still targeted on X. Read the selection-bias term and the naive gap. Does the naive gap equal ATT? Does ATT equal ATC?
  3. Raise a from 0 to 4 while watching the two dashed lines on the chart. Which numbers move — selection bias, ATT, ATC, or all three?
  4. Set b = 0 and a = 4. Selection bias is now large. What happened to the gap between ATT and ATC?
  5. Set b = −2 and a = 0, so the targeting rule picks the units who benefit least. Is there selection bias? Which is bigger, ATT or ATC?
  6. Press Assign at random and reseed a few times. What does randomization do to selection bias, and what does it do to the gap between ATT and ATC?

What you found

Treatment was targeted on X the whole time, but the selection-bias term went to zero the moment X stopped moving Y(0). ATT and ATC stayed apart, because the targeting rule still picked the units with the biggest effects. Those are two different things, and only one of them biases the naive comparison.

E[Y|D=1] − E[Y|D=0] = E[Y(1) − Y(0) | D=1] + ( E[Y(0)|D=1] − E[Y(0)|D=0] )

The first term is ATT. The second is selection bias, and it contains only Y(0): no Y(1), no τ, nothing about how the effect varies. Targeting on who benefits changes ATT relative to ATC — it does not, by itself, bias the naive gap for ATT. Targeting on who starts higher does.

Random assignment removes both: the treated and control groups become interchangeable in Y(0) and in τ, so the naive gap, ATT, ATC and ATE all coincide (up to the luck of the draw with ten units).

Assumptions and what is left out
  • Ten units with X = 1, 2, …, 10. Everything is deterministic: Y(0) = 100 + a·X, τ = b·X, Y(1) = Y(0) + τ. No noise.
  • Targeting treats exactly the k highest-X units. Random assignment treats five units chosen by a seeded shuffle.
  • Expectations are finite-population means over these ten units, so "ATT" here is the mean of τ among the treated, not a population parameter.
  • Left out on purpose: sampling variability, estimation from data, and conditioning on X (the conditional-independence route to recovering a causal effect from a targeted design). Each is its own artifact.

Where this leads: if X is observed, comparing treated and control units with the same X removes the Y(0) difference. That is the idea behind matching and regression adjustment.
