Turn the V dial

In 1970 the Capitol imposed a policy on District 13. To estimate its effect on GDP per capita, we build a synthetic District 13: a weighted average of two untreated districts, District 12 and District 2. The weight on District 12 is W; District 2 gets 1 − W.

Two loops choose W. Inner loop: given predictor weights V (how much each of four pre-1970 predictors counts), find the W* that best matches District 13 on those predictors. Outer loop: choose the V whose W* best reproduces District 13’s GDP in 1965 and 1969. You turn V by hand; the inner loop runs at once. Assumes you know what a weighted average is.

Turn the dials: how does V move W*?

There are four predictors, all pre-treatment: averages over 1961–1969, before the 1970 policy. They are GDP per capita, education, industrial share and unemployment.

Each predictor, used alone, has a target: the W that makes the synthetic district match District 13 exactly on that predictor. For GDP the target is 0.70: a blend of 70% District 12 and 30% District 2 gives 0.70 × 4.55 + 0.30 × 5.45 = 4.82, District 13’s value. Education’s target is also 0.70, and unemployment’s is 0.75.

Industrial share’s target is 1.53. District 13’s share (0.48) is higher than both donors’ (0.40 and 0.25). Matching it would take 153% weight on District 12 and −53% on District 2: 1.53 × 0.40 − 0.53 × 0.25 = 0.48. But weights can’t be negative and must add to 100%, so W must stay between 0 and 1. The target is out of reach: the closest any blend gets is W = 1 (all District 12), with an industrial share of 0.40.

For a given V, the loss at a W adds up each predictor’s gap between the synthetic district and District 13, in standard deviations, squared and multiplied by that predictor’s share of V. W* is the W with the smallest loss. Below, turn the dials and follow the four tasks under the chart; the table under the dials shows the loss at W*, predictor by predictor.

Try these, watching W* (the gold dot, and the number under the dials)

  1. Click the name GDP beside its dial: all of V goes on GDP, and W* lands on GDP’s target.
  2. Do the same for EDUC, UNEM and IND. Where does W* stop for industrial share, why not at 1.53, and why doesn’t the loss reach 0?
  3. Click GDP again, then turn the IND dial up a little at a time. How fast does W* move toward 1?
  4. Give GDP and IND the same weight (type the same number in both boxes) and set the others to 0. Is W* nearer GDP’s target or industry’s?

Where does W* land when every predictor counts equally?

In step 1 each predictor pulled W* toward its own target, and industrial share pulled hard. Now every predictor gets the same weight.

With V = 25% on each of the four predictors, where will W* be?

About 0.70, where GDP and education agree
About 0.72, the average of the three targets below 1
Above 0.75, pulled up by industrial share

The chart and the dials appear here once you lock in your prediction.

W* is the best W for the V you set: the weight on District 12 that brings the synthetic district closest to District 13 on the predictors, with each predictor’s gap counted according to its V. (Gaps are measured in standard deviations, so predictors on different scales can be compared.) Above 0.70, raising W makes the match worse on GDP and education, and past 0.75 on unemployment, but better on industrial share, whose gap keeps shrinking all the way to W = 1. With equal V, the best trade-off is W* = 0.82. Press Equal weights under the dials to see it.

Can any W close the industry gap?

Put all of V on industrial share, then look at the balance chart: the synthetic district (ring) can only sit on the bar between District 12 and District 2.

With all of V on industrial share, W* = 1 and the synthetic district still falls 0.08 short on industry. Why can no W close that gap?

District 13’s industrial share (0.48) is above both donors’ (0.40 and 0.25), and a weighted average with W between 0 and 1 can’t go above the higher donor
Industrial share is a fraction, so its gap counts for less in the loss than GDP’s does
The other three predictors still pull W* back toward 0.70
The dials move in 1% steps, too coarse to reach the target

How low can V push the error before 1970?

The outer score is the pre-period MSPE: the mean squared gap between District 13’s GDP and the synthetic district’s, in 1965 and 1969. Fitting W straight to those two years, ignoring the predictors, gives W = 0.67 and an MSPE of 0.

Is there a V that brings the MSPE down to 0?

Yes: the right V will lead the inner loop to W = 0.67
No: some fits can’t be reached through V

Turn the dials to get the MSPE as low as you can. Then press Search for V*, which tries every V on a 5% grid.

The search finds many V’s that tie for the lowest MSPE. What does the tie tell you?

V* is not unique, but every tied V gives the same W*, and the estimate depends only on W*
The grid is too coarse; a finer grid would find a single best V
The estimated effect of the policy differs across the tied V’s
All four predictors matter equally for GDP

Why can’t any V reach W = 0.67?

The chart marks the four targets, the range of W* that some V can reach, the W that fits GDP in 1965 and 1969 exactly, and your current W* (gold).

Why accept the worse fit? A synthetic district that tracks District 13 on past GDP and on the predictors is more likely to share the unmeasured things that drive GDP too. Fitting W to two years of GDP alone gives up that check.

Why does no V give W* = 0.67?

Every target is 0.70 or more, so below 0.70 raising W improves the match on all four predictors; for any V, the inner loop prefers 0.70 to 0.67
The search used a 5% grid, too coarse to find the V that gives 0.67
W can’t be negative, and 0.67 would need District 2 to have a negative weight
The outer score uses only two years, too few to pin down W

A fictional example set in Panem, the world of Suzanne Collins’s The Hunger Games; all numbers are invented. Predictors are 1961–1969 averages, in thousands for GDP per capita and as fractions for the rest. Predictors are divided by their standard deviation across the three districts before the inner loss is computed, as the Synth package does. The design follows Abadie, A. and Gardeazabal, J. (2003), “The Economic Costs of Conflict: A Case Study of the Basque Country,” American Economic Review 93(1): 112–132.
