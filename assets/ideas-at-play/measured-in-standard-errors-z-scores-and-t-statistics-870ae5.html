1. How spread out are averages?

2. How far from the mean, in SDs?

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value sits above (+) or below (−) the mean:

z = (value − mean) ÷ SD

3. How unusual is a sample's average?

For a sample mean, measure the distance in standard errors, the SD of the sampling distribution:

standard error = σ ÷ √n     z = (x̄ − μ) ÷ (σ ÷ √n)

4. What if σ is unknown?

The z for a sample mean needs σ, the SD of the whole population. In practice σ is almost never known, so we estimate it from the sample. That forces two changes:

1. Estimate σ with the sample SD, dividing by n − 1, not n:

s = √( Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) )

The deviations are measured from x̄, the sample's own mean, not from μ, and the sum of squared deviations from x̄ is never larger than the sum from μ. So dividing that sum by n would give a variance that is too small on average. Dividing by n − 1 corrects this exactly on average: s² is an unbiased estimate of σ². (R's sd() already divides by n − 1.)

2. Compare with the t-distribution, not the normal curve:

t = (x̄ − μ) ÷ (s ÷ √n),   df = n − 1

s changes from sample to sample, so t does not follow the normal curve. If the observations are independent draws from a normal population whose mean really is μ, t follows the t-distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom exactly; for other populations that is an approximation, which improves as n grows. The t-distribution assumes s was computed with n − 1, so the two changes go together.

You should now be able to:

  • say why averages of n vary less than single values, by σ ÷ √n (step 1);
  • turn a value or a sample mean into a z-score (steps 2 and 3);
  • choose z or t by asking whether σ is known; when it is not, do both things: compute s by dividing by n − 1, and use the t-distribution with df = n − 1 instead of the normal curve (steps 4 to 6).
Assumptions
  • dataAll numbers are made up: the lab test (μ = 250 ms, σ = 30 ms), the coffee sample (n = 9, x̄ = 232 ms, s = 26 ms), the app sample (n = 16, x̄ = 262 ms, s = 24 ms) and the café's 50 shots (mean 27 s, SD 4 s).
  • distributionReaction times are drawn as normal with mean 250 ms and SD 30 ms. Real reaction times are somewhat skewed right; the normal shape is a simplification.
  • simplificationPeople are sampled at random and independently, so the average's SD is σ ÷ √n. The t curve's shape assumes a roughly normal population, which matters most for small n.
  • simplificationIn step 1 both curves are scaled to the same peak height so their widths can be compared; steps 2 to 4 draw true densities.
  • parameterStep 1: n from 1 to 64 (default 4). Step 4: df from 1 to 30 (default 3); shares beyond ±2 are 2·P(T < −2), computed by numerical integration of the t density (R: 2*pt(-2, df)).

Left out: confidence intervals, hypothesis tests and p-values, which use the same z and t curves.
