# The movement toolkit: meetups, hackathons, and a brand kit

*Research notes, October 3, 2026, for milestone M5 ("The movement
toolkit, finished"). Not site copy, and nothing here is in Ben's voice.
It builds on `facilitation-assessment-social-learning-notes.md` (how
good facilitation works, which this file does not repeat) and
`pedagogy-and-showcase-notes.md` (Liberating Structures, the tuning
protocol, the hackathon study by Gama et al.).*

**What Ben asked for.** VISION.md: "a toolkit of sorts for sharing the
ideas about human-shaped software, proclaiming that your software is
human-shaped, or that would allow you to start an event or conversation
or community of your own." DECISIONS.md: "The event toolkit is for
meetups and Human-Shaped Hackathons: resources to lead conversations or
present with the principles front and center. It needs strong branding,
a logo, and well-crafted language." Also "No covenant" and "The arch".

**How sources are marked.** **Checked** means I opened the page in this
session; **from memory** means I did not, so check before quoting it in
public. Strength follows the earlier notes: these are practitioner
guides, so almost everything here is **Practice** (long use, no
controlled test), with the hackathon planning kit as the one piece of
research.

## 1. What well-run movement toolkits have in common

| Toolkit | What it gives a first-time organizer | Source | Lesson for us |
|---|---|---|---|
| P2PU learning circles | A repeated meeting shape (check-in, material, activity, reflection with "plus and delta"), the facilitator as "co-learner, not an expert", "keep quiet, especially at the beginning", redirect questions to the group, and wrap-up notes sent afterward. The first meeting decides how the group will run itself. | https://docs.p2pu.org/facilitation/facilitation-basics and https://docs.p2pu.org/methodology/learning-circle-structure (Checked) | The organizer does not need to know how to build. Give them the shape and the words, and say plainly that they are a host, not a teacher. |
| The Carpentries | Separate checklists for the host and the instructor, each split into before, during, and after: confirm the room is accessible, one helper per 8 to 10 learners, email setup instructions in advance, food, sticky notes as status flags, minute cards at lunch and at the end, a survey before people leave. | https://docs.carpentries.org/resources/workshops/checklists.html and https://carpentries.github.io/workshop-template/ (Checked) | A checklist in three parts (before, on the day, after) is the most copied piece of any workshop kit. Setup instructions go out days before, never on the morning. |
| Global Day of Coderetreat | A whole day built on practice rather than output: 45-minute sessions, a 15-minute retrospective after each, code deleted after every session, a problem chosen so that nobody can finish it, and a closing circle with three questions (what did you learn, what surprised you, what will you do differently). "You are facilitating a discussion, not lecturing a group." | https://www.coderetreat.org/facilitators/facilitating/ and https://www.coderetreat.org/about/ (Checked) | The nearest existing proof that a day of building can count learning as the result, and that people come back for it. We keep the app (it is theirs) but borrow the retrospective rhythm and the closing circle. |
| Code for America Brigade Organizer's Playbook (2015) | A checklist for hack nights (regular day and time, a venue, an online invite, partners invited, food and AV), and key questions: how will we welcome new people, how will the work continue from one hack night to the next, what research can prepare attendees. Before a hack night, a workshop with partners writes "how might we..." statements so people arrive with real needs. | https://hackathon-planning-kit.org/files/code-for-America-playbook.pdf, pp. 3 to 10 (Checked) | Regularity beats size. Bring the problem, not the solution. Ask, before the event, how the work continues after it. |
| Mozilla Maker Party and Mozilla Clubs | Event guides sized for small (2 to 5), medium (5 to 50), and large groups; activities that work without internet; graphics to publicize the event; a kit that runs from a kickoff meeting through recruitment, facilitation ("make people feel welcome"), and a debrief that shares what was made and learned. | https://blog.mozilla.org/foundation-archive/mozilla-learning/maker-party-2014-resources-for-libraries-and-learning-spaces/ and https://learning.mozilla.org/en-US/events/resources (search summaries, Checked as listings) | Size the guide to a small room first. Ship the announcement graphics with the guide. End every event with a debrief that goes somewhere public. |
| Mozilla Open Leaders | One real project each, a weekly cohort call, a mentor call every two weeks, and a final call where everyone shares a case study. | In the facilitation notes, 2.5 | The show-and-tell at the end is the event's real output. |
| Liberating Structures | 1-2-4-All: one minute alone, two in pairs, five in fours, seven all together, so everyone has an answer before anyone speaks. What, So What, Now What for the close. | http://www.liberatingstructures.com/1-2-4-all (Checked) | Use 1-2-4-All in the meetup's middle, where a room of strangers needs to talk without the loudest person going first. |
| Creative Commons | Official logos and badges downloadable as SVG, EPS, PNG, and GIF, with print advice (use vector files for print), and a policy: descriptive use is free, modified marks are not allowed (except a color change that keeps 3:1 contrast), and nothing may imply endorsement. | https://creativecommons.org/mission/downloads/ and https://creativecommons.org/policies/ (Checked) | A movement can give its marks away freely and still ask that they not be altered, because recognition depends on sameness. |

**What they share.** Every one of these is written for a person who has
never run the event before, and none of them asks the organizer to be
an expert. Each gives: a fixed shape with timings; the first and last
words, or a script for them; a before, on the day, and after checklist;
text to announce it; and somewhere to send what happened. That is the
outline both guides follow.

## 2. What makes a meetup or hackathon guide usable by a first-time organizer

From the toolkits above, plus the planning research:

- **Nolte et al., "How to organize a hackathon: a planning kit"**
  (2020; revised as "How to organize an in-person, online or hybrid
  hackathon", arXiv 2008.08025v3), https://arxiv.org/html/2008.08025v3
  (Checked). Organized around twelve decisions: goal, theme, competition
  or cooperation, stakeholders, recruitment, preparation, duration and
  breaks, ideation, team formation, agenda, mentoring, and continuity.
  Two findings matter here: the goal has to be decided "before any
  planning of the event begins", and a competitive format suits events
  meant to produce innovations, while cooperation suits events for
  "learning about programming tools" or a civic cause; low-value prizes
  or none suit events whose goals are "learning, expanding social ties,
  or attracting newcomers". Research synthesis. Moderate.
- **Gama et al. (2025)**, a one-day vibe-coding hackathon (in the
  pedagogy notes, 6.1): fast prototypes, but "premature convergence in
  ideation" and little engagement with engineering practice. So the day
  starts with writing the why and naming the people before any code,
  which stage 00 already asks.
- **Against ranking.** Practitioner essays argue that judging is
  subjective, that judges tire through hours of demos, that mixed
  experience makes ranking unfair, and that people build what they
  think will win (https://www.steventammen.com/old-posts/on-hackathons-and-judging-them/,
  https://medium.com/@yaronsadka/hackathons-beyond-the-prizes-764c002c70d3,
  search summaries, Checked as listings). Weak on their own; they agree
  with the stronger evidence in the earlier notes that visible ranking
  lowers motivation through social comparison (Hanus and Fox 2015) and
  that rewards steer behavior toward the reward (Deci, Koestner, and
  Ryan 1999). This is the "why" for no prizes for speed or polish.
- **Accessibility, listed, not requested.** Event accessibility guides
  agree on step-free access, accessible restrooms, a quiet room, breaks,
  captions or a remote option, materials in advance, and naming what is
  available in the announcement rather than waiting for people to ask
  (ABA Commission on Disability Rights, Accessible Meetings and Events
  Toolkit; EARN, Planning an Accessible Meeting or Event checklist;
  https://www.staff.ncl.ac.uk/christian.perfect/accessibility-checklist-for-events/checklist.html;
  search summaries, Checked as listings). Practice.

**Usable means:** one page per event, a time column that adds up, the
organizer's words written out for the two moments people freeze (the
opening and the close), materials linked rather than described, an
announcement to copy, and a short list for afterward. Everything else
is optional and should say so.

## 3. What brand kits include

| Kit | Files | Rules | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| GitHub | A zip of the Invertocat, the wordmark lockup, and product marks. | Only black, white, and occasionally gray or green; don't rearrange, add effects, place on busy backgrounds, stretch, recolor, or imply endorsement. "Large, with high contrast and no other effects." | https://brand.github.com/foundations/logo (Checked) |
| Creative Commons | SVG, EPS, PNG, GIF; vector for print. | No modified marks; one allowed color change with 3:1 contrast; event names need permission. | Above (Checked) |
| General guides | Clear space measured from a part of the mark itself (a letter's height or the icon's unit); minimum size in pixels for screens and millimeters for print. | | https://robotcreative.com/blog/your-logo-needs-space-blog/ and similar (search summaries) |

**What ours needs.** The wordmark (light and dark, plus one-color black
and white for photocopied flyers, since a meetup flyer is often
photocopied), the icon, the two declaration marks, PNG copies for slide
tools that will not take SVG (Google Slides is one; from memory), the
colors with their values, the typeface with its license, and four rules:
the arch rule, clear space, minimum size, and don't recolor or rebuild
it. Every file is drawn with outlined letters so it works without the
font, generated by a script so it can be rebuilt.

**One choice that differs from the others.** Creative Commons asks
permission before an event uses its name. The ideas here are free and
DECISIONS.md has no covenant, so the default drafted into the kit is
that anyone may call an event a Human-Shaped Meetup or Hackathon without
asking, as long as it keeps the parts that make it one. That is a
decision for Ben (see the report for M5).
